# Feedback science for a "fix my grammar / check my tone" coach feature (evidence sheet, as of 17 Nov 2026)

How to read the labels:
- **(verified)**: authors, year, title, venue, volume and pages were checked against the DOI's Crossref record in this session (arXiv, Research Square and World Bank items were checked against their own records). Effect sizes come from the abstract or from the source named, unless noted.
- **(check)**: the bibliographic details or the numbers come only from a secondary source, a search snippet or a machine summary (Semantic Scholar "TLDR"). Confirm them before quoting.
- "Revision" means accuracy when correcting the same text. "New text" means accuracy in a later, different piece of writing (transfer). "Delayed" means a delayed post-test, usually 1 to 10 weeks later. Revision gains are not the same as learning.

## 1. Written corrective feedback (WCF): does correcting grammar teach, and which kind works?

### Takeaway
Across 50+ controlled studies, WCF improves L2 grammatical accuracy by a moderate amount, and the gains persist in new texts written weeks later. Truscott's "abandon it" position is now a minority view. Direct, indirect and metalinguistic feedback come out roughly equal at the meta-analytic level. Only a few studies separate them: direct correction helps more with grammar and indirect (locate-only) feedback with non-grammar errors, while focused feedback and short rule explanations help with single rule-governed features. Accuracy gains in revision are consistently larger than, and do not predict, gains in new writing.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses and syntheses**
- Kang & Han (2015), *Modern Language Journal* 99(1), 1–18, 21 primary studies: WCF "can lead to greater grammatical accuracy in second language writing", but its effect depends on learner proficiency, setting and writing genre. The exact pooled g was not confirmed in this session (one secondary source calls it "small to moderate"). Direct and focused feedback trended larger than indirect and unfocused, but the difference was not significant. — [Kang & Han 2015, doi:10.1111/modl.12189](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12189) (verified); direct-vs-indirect note via a [2024 review cited in search results](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/LEARN/article/view/274068) (check)
- Liu & Brown (2015), *JSLW* 30, 66–81: a methodological synthesis of 32 published studies and 12 dissertations. It found four recurring weaknesses: poor reporting; low ecological validity (one-shot treatments, timed in-class writing); "bundled" feedback types that cannot be separated; and inconsistent accuracy measures. — [Liu & Brown 2015, doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2015.08.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2015.08.011) (verified bibliographic; content via [Scinapse abstract](https://scinapse.io/papers/1842595263))
- Brown, Liu & Norouzian, Bayesian meta-analysis, *Language Teaching Research* (online 2023; print 2026, 30(3), 1357–1389): 52 control-group studies. It shows "robust evidence of the durability of moderate effectiveness of WCF over time", and is the first meta-analysis to separate short-, medium- and long-term effects. Direct, indirect and metalinguistic WCF were "all yielding similar effect sizes". Exact g values were not retrieved. — [Brown, Liu & Norouzian, doi:10.1177/13621688221147374](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221147374) (verified)
- Lim & Renandya (2020), *TESL-EJ* 24(3), 1–26: a moderate overall effect of WCF on L2 grammatical accuracy, with direct feedback larger than indirect but not significantly. The numeric g was not retrieved; one secondary source attributes g = 0.59 across 35 studies to "Chen and Renandya (2020)", which is probably a mis-citation. — [NIE repository record](https://repository.nie.edu.sg/handle/10497/22307) (check)
- Mao, Lee & Li (2024), *Language Teaching* 57(4), 449–477: a synthesis of 50 *naturalistic* classroom WCF studies, where feedback was not manipulated. It covers four themes: teacher practices, learner responses, stakeholder beliefs, and motivation/emotion. These studies give no effect sizes, but they cover real-classroom engagement. — [Mao, Lee & Li 2024, doi:10.1017/s0261444823000393](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0261444823000393) (verified)
- Sheng & Ganapathy (2026), *Language Teaching Research Quarterly*: a systematic review of 54 studies (2010–2026). WCF and dynamic WCF consistently improved accuracy, with mixed effects on complexity and fluency. Most designs were quasi-experimental pre/post. — [Sheng & Ganapathy 2026, doi:10.32038/ltrq.2026.54.07](https://doi.org/10.32038/ltrq.2026.54.07) (check)
- Peer feedback (Vuogan & Li 2023, *TESOL Quarterly* 57(4), 1115–1138), 26 studies: d = .73 [.54, .92]. Effects were larger on *revisions than on new compositions*, and larger on content than on language (lexis/grammar). It found no significant difference between peer feedback, teacher feedback and self-revision (few comparisons). — [Vuogan & Li 2023, doi:10.1002/tesq.3178](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.3178) (verified)

**L1 (first-language) writing**
- Graham, Hebert & Harris (2015), grades 1–8, true and quasi-experiments: feedback on writing improved quality. Effect sizes were 0.87 from adults, 0.58 from peers, 0.62 from self-assessment and 0.38 from computers. Teacher monitoring alone and the 6+1 Trait model did not meaningfully help. — [Graham, Hebert & Harris 2015, *Elementary School Journal* 115(4), 523–547, doi:10.1086/681947](https://doi.org/10.1086/681947) (verified)
- Graham & Perin (2007), grades 4–12, 154 effect sizes on writing quality: traditional grammar instruction gave −0.32 and sentence combining gave 0.50. Other effects: strategy instruction 0.82, summarization 0.82, product goals 0.70, word processing 0.55, study of models 0.25. Explicit grammar lessons detached from the student's own writing did not improve L1 writing quality. — [Graham & Perin 2007, *J. Educ. Psych.* 99(3), 445–476, doi:10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445) (verified)

**The Truscott vs Ferris debate**
- Truscott (1996) argued that grammar correction in L2 writing classes is ineffective, possibly harmful, and should be abandoned. — [Truscott 1996, *Language Learning* 46(2), 327–369, doi:10.1111/j.1467-1770.1996.tb01238.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1996.tb01238.x) (verified)
- Ferris replied that the evidence was too thin and too mixed to abandon correction (1999). She later set out the open research questions and interim practice (2004). — [Ferris 1999, *JSLW* 8(1), 1–11, doi:10.1016/S1060-3743(99)80110-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(99)80110-6) (verified); [Ferris 2004, *JSLW* 13(1), 49–62, doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2004.04.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2004.04.005) (verified)
- Truscott (2007) meta-analysed effects on *new writing*. His best estimate was that correction has a small negative effect on the ability to write accurately, and that any real benefit is very small (95% confidence). — [Truscott 2007, *JSLW* 16(4), 255–272, doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2007.06.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2007.06.003) (verified bibliographic; finding via Semantic Scholar TLDR: check)
- Truscott & Hsu (2008): two groups differed sharply in error reduction during *revision* but were indistinguishable in later *new writing*. Successful revision did not predict learning (uncoded feedback). — [Truscott & Hsu 2008, *JSLW* 17(4), 292–305, doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2008.05.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2008.05.003) (verified bibliographic; finding via Semantic Scholar TLDR: check)

**Direct vs indirect; comprehensive vs focused**
- Van Beuningen, De Jong & Kuiken (2012), N = 268 Dutch secondary L2 learners, comprehensive (all-error) feedback. Direct and indirect CF both beat self-editing without CF and extra writing practice without CF. This held in revision and in *new texts* 1 and 4 weeks later. Only direct CF improved grammatical accuracy in new writing; indirect CF helped non-grammatical (e.g. lexical, orthographic) accuracy most. CF did not make students simplify their writing (complexity and lexical diversity held). — [Van Beuningen et al. 2012, *Language Learning* 62(1), 1–41, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00674.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00674.x) (verified)
- Karim & Nassaji (2020), N = 53 intermediate ESL, comprehensive WCF over 3 weeks plus a writing task 2 weeks later. All three feedback types (direct, underline-only, underline+metalinguistic cue) significantly beat the control in *revision*. Gains in *new* writing (direct; underline+metalinguistic) were "largely non-significant". — [Karim & Nassaji 2020, *LTR* 24(4), 519–539, doi:10.1177/1362168818802469](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168818802469) (verified)
- Suzuki, Nassaji & Sato (2019), N = 88 Japanese EFL; direct vs indirect, each with or without metalinguistic explanation; targets were articles and past perfect. Both WCF types improved revision for both structures. Improvement in *new* writing appeared only for the past perfect. Explicitness partly affected revision but not new writing. — [Suzuki et al. 2019, *System* 81, 135–145, doi:10.1016/j.system.2018.12.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2018.12.017) (verified)
- Chandler (2003): direct correction and simple underlining were both significantly better than describing the error type (even with underlining) at reducing long-term error. — [Chandler 2003, *JSLW* 12(3), 267–296, doi:10.1016/S1060-3743(03)00038-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(03)00038-9) (verified bibliographic; finding via Semantic Scholar TLDR: check)
- Ferris & Roberts (2001): both feedback groups beat the no-feedback group on a self-editing task. Coded ("this is a verb-tense error") and uncoded (underline only) groups did not differ. Locating the error was what mattered; the label added little. — [Ferris & Roberts 2001, *JSLW* 10(3), 161–184, doi:10.1016/S1060-3743(01)00039-X](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(01)00039-X) (verified bibliographic; finding via Semantic Scholar TLDR: check)
- Sheen, Wright & Moldawa (2009): unfocused CF was of limited value, while focused CF improved grammatical accuracy. — [Sheen et al. 2009, *System* 37(4), 556–569, doi:10.1016/j.system.2009.09.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2009.09.002) (verified bibliographic; finding via TLDR: check)
- Ellis, Sheen, Murakami & Takashima (2008), Japanese EFL, English articles: written CF was effective. Its focused vs unfocused comparison is often summarised as showing no clear difference, but that was not confirmed in this session. — [Ellis et al. 2008, *System* 36(3), 353–371, doi:10.1016/j.system.2008.02.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2008.02.001) (verified bibliographic; detail: check)
- Bitchener & Knoch (2008), N = 144 migrant and international ESL students, 2 months, English articles: WCF groups beat the no-feedback group, and accuracy was retained over 7 weeks. — [Bitchener & Knoch 2008, *LTR* 12(3), 409–431, doi:10.1177/1362168808089924](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168808089924) (verified)
- Bitchener & Knoch (2010), N = 52 low-intermediate ESL, **10 months**, five texts (pre, immediate and three delayed tests). All three treatments beat the control on every post-test: direct CF + written + oral metalinguistic explanation; direct CF + written explanation; direct CF only. The three treatments **did not differ**, so here the extra explanation added nothing over direct correction for a simple feature. — [Bitchener & Knoch 2010, *Applied Linguistics* 31(2), 193–214, doi:10.1093/applin/amp016](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amp016) (verified)
- Related: Bitchener & Knoch (2009), *System* 37(2), 322–329, on direct WCF types — [doi:10.1016/j.system.2008.12.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2008.12.006) (verified bibliographic only); Bitchener, Young & Cameron (2005), *JSLW* 14(3), 191–205, on direct feedback plus a 5-minute conference — [doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2005.08.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2005.08.001) (verified bibliographic only; findings not retrieved)

**Metalinguistic explanation (ME)**
- Sheen (2007), N = 91 adult intermediate ESL, articles. Direct correction and direct + metalinguistic correction both beat the control immediately. Only **direct + metalinguistic** stayed ahead on *delayed* tests. Gains correlated with language-analytic aptitude, more strongly in the metalinguistic group. — [Sheen 2007, *TESOL Quarterly* 41(2), 255–283, doi:10.1002/j.1545-7249.2007.tb00059.x](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2007.tb00059.x) (verified)
- Shintani & Ellis (2013), English indefinite article: direct CF had no effect on accurate use. Whole-class ME improved *explicit* knowledge, but the effect was not durable and probably did not reach implicit knowledge. — [Shintani & Ellis 2013, *JSLW* 22(3), 286–306, doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2013.03.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2013.03.011) (verified bibliographic; finding via TLDR: check)
- Shintani, Ellis & Suzuki (2014), Japanese university students; targets were articles and the hypothetical conditional, measured in **new writing**. Feedback helped the conditional but not the article. Direct CF lasted longer than ME, and **giving the chance to rewrite strengthened the effect**: direct CF followed by revision was best overall. — [Shintani et al. 2014, *Language Learning* 64(1), 103–131, doi:10.1111/lang.12029](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12029) (verified)

**Self-correction and error logs / error awareness**
- Lalande (1982), intermediate learners of German. The experimental group got coded errors to self-correct, an **Error Awareness Sheet** tracking each student's error frequencies by category, and problem-solving techniques. It outperformed a control group (traditional correction plus grammar review) in most non-lexical error categories; the control group's errors rose. Sample sizes and effect sizes were not retrieved. — [Lalande 1982, *Modern Language Journal* 66(2), 140–149, doi:10.1111/j.1540-4781.1982.tb06973.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1982.tb06973.x) (verified bibliographic; results via [abstract record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/reducing-composition-errors-an-experiment/813370004132069377-10811): check)
- Ferris, Liu, Sinha & Senna (2013), "Written corrective feedback for individual L2 writers": case-study work on individualised WCF. — [Ferris et al. 2013, *JSLW* 22(3), 307–329, doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2012.09.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2012.09.009) (verified bibliographic; findings not retrieved in this session)
- Ellis's (2009) typology of WCF types (direct, indirect, metalinguistic, focused/unfocused, electronic, reformulation) is the standard vocabulary for describing feedback designs. — [Ellis 2009, *ELT Journal* 63(2), 97–107, doi:10.1093/elt/ccn023](https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/ccn023) (verified; Crossref lists the online year as 2008)

### Inferences
- The most robust single message: **fixing the draft is not the learning event**. Revision accuracy rises under almost any feedback, but transfer to new texts is smaller, slower and error-type dependent. A coach that measures success by "errors fixed" will overestimate learning. The meaningful metric is error rates in the learner's *next* texts.
- Locate-only feedback (underline) appears about as effective as labelled feedback (Ferris & Roberts) and better than describing the error type (Chandler). That favours a cheap "here's where; try it" step over long labels. Direct correction seems safer for grammar the learner cannot yet produce (Van Beuningen) and for complex structures (Shintani et al. 2014).
- A short rule explanation helps most for **single, rule-governed features** and for learners with analytic aptitude (Sheen 2007). It added nothing in Bitchener & Knoch (2010) and faded in Shintani & Ellis (2013). The explanation should therefore be one or two lines, attached to a recurring pattern, not to every correction.
- Focused feedback (a few recurring categories) is better supported for *learning* than comprehensive feedback. Comprehensive correction is still useful for producing a clean draft (Van Beuningen). This supports a dual output: fix everything silently, but teach only one to three patterns per session.
- Lalande's Error Awareness Sheet is the closest historical precedent for a cross-session "personal error profile". The evidence for it is old and small, and it comes bundled with coded feedback and self-correction, so the log itself has not been tested in isolation.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes for Kang & Han (2015), Lim & Renandya (2020) and Brown et al. (short/medium/long-term g) were not retrievable from abstracts; the full texts are needed.
- I found no RCT that isolates the effect of an *error log or profile* (without coded feedback or self-correction bundled in) on new-text accuracy.
- Ferris's "treatable vs untreatable error" distinction and Chandler's finding on "requiring students to correct" were not confirmed in this session.
- Most WCF studies target a few English features (articles, past tense, conditionals) in short, timed texts with intermediate L2 learners. Evidence for L1 writers' grammar, for word choice, and for long-form or genre writing is thin.

## 2. Noticing and output: does making the learner find or produce the fix matter?

### Takeaway
Theory (Schmidt's noticing hypothesis; Swain's output hypothesis) holds that learners must notice the gap between what they wrote and the target form. Producing language pushes them to notice. Empirically, the *quality* of noticing matters: understanding why beats merely registering a change, and learners' depth of engagement predicts retention. Making the learner locate or self-repair produces more durable, transferable gains in a few studies, including the first AI-feedback RCT on this question (a preprint). It costs more effort and does not always improve every outcome.

### Cited Findings
- Schmidt (1990) proposed that conscious attention to (noticing of) linguistic features is required for input to become intake. This underpins designs that highlight errors. — [Schmidt 1990, *Applied Linguistics* 11(2), 129–158, doi:10.1093/applin/11.2.129](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129) (verified)
- Swain & Lapkin (1995) showed that problems learners hit while producing output trigger cognitive processes (noticing gaps, hypothesis testing) that support L2 learning. — [Swain & Lapkin 1995, *Applied Linguistics* 16(3), 371–391, doi:10.1093/applin/16.3.371](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/16.3.371) (verified)
- Qi & Lapkin (2001), a three-stage writing task (write, compare with a reformulation, revise): promoting the **quality** of noticing (understanding-level rather than mere registration) matters more than promoting noticing as such. — [Qi & Lapkin 2001, *JSLW* 10(4), 277–303, doi:10.1016/S1060-3743(01)00046-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(01)00046-7) (verified bibliographic; finding via TLDR: check)
- Sachs & Polio (2007), 15 learners in a within-subjects study, then 54 with a control group, on a write-compare-revise task. **Error correction beat reformulation** (a native-like rewrite of the whole text) for revision accuracy in both experiments. Reported noticing tracked revision accuracy. Think-aloud was reactive. — [Sachs & Polio 2007, *SSLA* 29(1), 67–100, doi:10.1017/S0272263107070039](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263107070039) (verified)
- Storch & Wigglesworth (2010), pairs receiving reformulation (direct) or editing symbols (indirect), with rewriting on day 5 and an individual retest on day 28. Uptake and retention depended on the error type and, "more importantly", on learners' attitudes, beliefs and goals as shown in their talk about the feedback. — [Storch & Wigglesworth 2010, *SSLA* 32(2), 303–334, doi:10.1017/S0272263109990532](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263109990532) (verified)
- Hanaoka (2007), a four-stage writing task on learners' spontaneous attention to form via output and model texts. — [Hanaoka 2007, *LTR* 11(4), 459–479, doi:10.1177/1362168807080963](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168807080963) (verified bibliographic; findings not retrieved)
- **AI-specific, locating vs fixing:** Lu, Zhang & Li (2026, preprint, not peer-reviewed), an RCT with 70 Mandarin-speaking learners on English articles (66 at day 7; 60 per-protocol; 3,264 item responses). *Socratic* GenAI feedback, which guided self-repair, beat *direct-answer* feedback on delayed near-transfer accuracy (OR = 1.80, 95% CI [1.40, 2.30]) and on far-transfer production (adjusted difference = 0.402, p_FDR = .040). It produced *greater cognitive load* but much less immediate overconfidence. Delayed retention of trained items and timed grammaticality judgment did not differ. — [Lu et al. 2026, Research Square, doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-10545020/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10545020/v1) (verified as preprint record)
- Özturan & Uysal (2025), a quasi-experiment with two EFL university classes. **Mediation-based** feedback (dialogic, graduated hints matched to the learner's zone of proximal development) beat teacher direct correction on linguistic accuracy with a large between-group effect. Direct correction produced small gains in accuracy but sustained medium gains in rhetoric, where the mediation group's gains regressed. — [Özturan & Uysal 2025, *TESOL in Context* 34(2), doi:10.21153/tesol2025vol34no2art2211](https://doi.org/10.21153/tesol2025vol34no2art2211) (verified)
- Indirect (locate-only) evidence from Section 1 also applies here: underlining matched direct correction for long-term error reduction ([Chandler 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(03)00038-9)), and codes added nothing over underlining ([Ferris & Roberts 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(01)00039-X)).

### Inferences
- A good minimal loop: **flag location, let the learner attempt, then reveal**. "Attempt" can be a single tap ("I see it" / "show me") so it is not slow. The Socratic preprint and the mediation studies suggest that the self-repair step drives transfer and calibration. It adds load, so it should be applied only to the one to three focus patterns, not to every error.
- Whole-text reformulation (the default LLM behaviour: "here is your improved text") is the weakest noticing condition in the Sachs & Polio comparison. If a rewrite is shown, the changed spans should be explicitly marked so the learner can see the gap.
- Asking a one-line "why do you think this changed?" or "what's the rule?" targets understanding-level noticing (Qi & Lapkin). Learner beliefs and goals strongly gate uptake (Storch & Wigglesworth), so the coach should ask or remember whether the user wants to learn or just wants a clean text.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed RCT yet compares "AI fixes" vs "AI locates, learner fixes" on *new-text* accuracy over weeks. Lu et al. (2026) is a preprint on a single grammatical feature with item-level tests, not free writing.
- Noticing studies are mostly small (N = 15–90) and measure revision or short-term retention.

## 3. Tone, register and pragmatics: teachable? how? does comparing versions help?

### Takeaway
L2 pragmatics (politeness, request and apology strategies, formality) is clearly teachable, with medium to very large effects. **Explicit** metapragmatic instruction beats implicit exposure. Formulaic elements (openings, closings, set phrases) are picked up fastest; freer, interpersonal choices develop gradually and non-linearly. Comparing contrasting cases yields about d = .50. It works best when learners look for similarities or differences and the principle is stated *after* the comparison. AI rewrites can quietly push tone toward one cultural norm, often more positive or more Western.

### Cited Findings
- Jeon & Kaya (2006), a meta-analysis of 13 pre-2003 studies in Norris & Ortega (eds.), *Synthesizing Research on Language Learning and Teaching* (John Benjamins), pp. 165–211. Against controls, explicit instruction gave d = 0.70 and implicit gave d = 0.44; pre-post figures were explicit 1.9 and implicit 1.01. — via [Shakki 2022, *Frontiers in Psychology* 13, 1016661, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1016661](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1016661) (Shakki: verified; Jeon & Kaya details: check)
- Plonsky & Zhuang (2019), a meta-analysis of 50 studies in Taguchi (ed.), *Routledge Handbook of SLA and Pragmatics*, pp. 287–307: overall d = 1.52. — via [Shakki 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1016661) (check)
- Yousefi & Nassaji (2019), *ITL – International Journal of Applied Linguistics* 170, 277–308, 39 studies (2006–2016) on instruction and corrective feedback in pragmatics: computer-assisted instruction showed larger effects than face-to-face. — via [Shakki 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1016661) and an earlier [conference version on Zenodo, doi:10.5281/zenodo.1474336](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1474336) (check: the DOI I tried for the journal article did not resolve)
- Derakhshan & Shakki (2021), 17 studies of request instruction in the Iranian context: g = 1.48 overall; explicit g = 1.53 vs implicit g = 1.20. These are single-context data with very large values, so treat them as inflated. — via [Shakki 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1016661) (check)
- Taguchi (2015), a review of 58 instructional intervention studies in interlanguage pragmatics, asking whether instruction works and which methods are most effective. — [Taguchi 2015, *Language Teaching* 48(1), 1–50, doi:10.1017/S0261444814000263](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444814000263) (verified)
- Keller, Trüb, Horbach, Jansen & Fleckenstein (2025), N = 322 Swiss grade 8–9 EFL students, three (semi-)formal request-email tasks in an online genre-based environment. Uptake occurred in all seven genre elements. The largest gains were in task completion and **formulaic** elements (e.g. "Hi/Hello" giving way to "Dear Ms …"). Freer interpersonal elements, such as succinct self-introduction and a clear statement of purpose, improved more gradually and with some backsliding. — [Keller et al. 2025, *RISTAL* 8(1), 27–46, doi:10.2478/ristal-2025-0005](https://doi.org/10.2478/ristal-2025-0005) (verified)
- Klipp & Reinders (2025), a small quasi-experiment on computer-assisted CF for L2 pragmatics: feedback with only an affective pedagogical-agent component outperformed high-information text feedback. — [Klipp & Reinders 2025, *Digital Applied Linguistics*, doi:10.29140/dal.v3.102515](https://doi.org/10.29140/dal.v3.102515) (check)
- **Contrasting cases:** Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013), 57 experiments and 336 tests. Case comparison beat sequential, single-case, non-analogous cases and traditional instruction: d = .50, 95% CI [.44, .56]. Reliable moderators: asking learners to find **similarities**, giving the **principle after** the comparison, perceptual content, and immediate testing. — [Alfieri et al. 2013, *Educational Psychologist* 48(2), 87–113, doi:10.1080/00461520.2013.775712](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712) (verified)
- Schwartz & Bransford (1998), "A time for telling": analysing contrasting cases prepares learners to learn more from subsequent explanation ("telling"). — [Schwartz & Bransford 1998, *Cognition and Instruction* 16(4), 475–522, doi:10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4) (verified)
- Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003): comparing two cases (analogical encoding) improves transfer of the underlying principle compared with studying cases separately. — [Gentner et al. 2003, *J. Educ. Psych.* 95(2), 393–408, doi:10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393) (verified)
- Nicol (2021): students generate *internal* feedback by comparing their work with reference information. Teachers should make these natural comparisons explicit and deliberate, e.g. comparing one's own work with exemplars or alternative versions. — [Nicol 2021, *Assessment & Evaluation in HE* 46(5), 756–778, doi:10.1080/02602938.2020.1823314](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2020.1823314) (verified)
- **AI shifts tone and cultural style:** Agarwal, Naaman & Vashistha (2025), N = 118 participants from India and the US doing culturally grounded writing with and without AI suggestions. AI gave Americans larger efficiency gains and led Indian participants to adopt Western writing styles, changing "not just what is written but also how it is written". — [Agarwal et al. 2025, CHI '25, doi:10.1145/3706598.3713564](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713564) (verified)
- Hohenstein et al. (2023), two randomized experiments on AI "smart replies": they increased communication speed and positive emotional language, and partners rated each other as closer and more cooperative. People *suspected* of using AI replies were rated more negatively. — [Hohenstein et al. 2023, *Scientific Reports* 13, 5487, doi:10.1038/s41598-023-30938-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-30938-9) (verified)

### Inferences
- "Check my tone" is a pragmatics-instruction opportunity with strong evidence for the explicit route: name the audience, the relationship and the move (request, refusal, apology), and say briefly *why* a phrasing reads as too blunt or too stiff.
- The natural AI format for tone is a **contrasting-case pair or triplet**: the user's version vs one or two alternatives at different formality or directness levels. Ask the user to pick which fits the reader *before* stating the principle; that ordering is the moderator favoured in Alfieri et al. and Schwartz & Bransford. This also returns the choice to the user, which protects voice and ownership (Section 6).
- Formulaic elements (salutations, closings, hedges such as "Could you possibly…", softeners) are quick wins worth adding to the error and tone profile. Freer interpersonal choices need repeated exposure across sessions.
- Because AI suggestions homogenise toward Western or positive registers, tone feedback should be framed relative to the user's stated audience and culture, not to a single "professional" norm. It should also be able to say "your original is fine for this reader".

### Gaps
- Pragmatics meta-analytic numbers here come from a secondary review (Shakki 2022); the primary chapters were not accessed. Effect sizes are probably inflated by small, pre-post and single-context studies.
- I found no peer-reviewed experiment on *LLM-delivered* tone or register feedback and its effect on learners' later independent writing. A 2026 study ([Tenzer, Abidi & Feuerriegel, *Management International Review*, doi:10.1007/s11575-026-00637-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11575-026-00637-4), check) examines LLM *translation* cultural sensitivity under different prompts, not learner learning.
- Very little research covers register teaching for L1 adult writers (e.g. workplace email tone); the evidence is almost all L2.

## 4. General feedback research: what kind of feedback helps, and when does it hurt?

### Takeaway
Feedback has an average effect of about d = 0.4 to 0.5, with enormous variance. It works when it carries **information about the task and how to improve** (high-information, elaborated feedback: d ≈ 0.5 to 1.0). Bare right/wrong verification has almost no effect (d ≈ 0.05). It hurts in more than a third of cases, especially when it pulls attention toward the self (praise, grading, ego) rather than the task. Modern frameworks stress building the learner's own ability to judge quality, through comparison, self-assessment and feedback literacy.

### Cited Findings
- Hattie & Timperley (2007): a model of feedback that answers "Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next?" (feed up, back, forward) at four levels: task, process, self-regulation and self. Feedback at the self level (praise) is least effective. Its impact "can be either positive or negative". — [Hattie & Timperley 2007, *RER* 77(1), 81–112, doi:10.3102/003465430298487](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487) (verified)
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), 435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000: overall d = 0.48. By type: high-information feedback d = 0.99 [0.82, 1.15] (k = 42); corrective feedback d = 0.46 (k = 238); reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24 (k = 39). By outcome: cognitive d = 0.51, motor d = 0.63, motivational d = 0.33. Controlled designs gave d = 0.42 vs pre-post d = 0.63. Heterogeneity was high in all subgroups; timing and the Hattie–Timperley levels were not coded as moderators. — [Wisniewski et al. 2020, *Frontiers in Psychology* 10, 3087, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087) (verified; moderator numbers from the full text, Table 3)
- Kluger & DeNisi (1996), 607 effect sizes and 23,663 observations: feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41), but **more than one-third decreased performance**. Effectiveness falls as feedback moves attention up the hierarchy from task learning toward meta-task and self-related processes. — [Kluger & DeNisi 1996, *Psychological Bulletin* 119(2), 254–284, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) (verified)
- Shute (2008): formative feedback should be non-evaluative, supportive, timely and specific. It comes as verification, explanation, hints or worked examples, and its success interacts with learner characteristics and task. The review ends with guidelines. — [Shute 2008, *RER* 78(1), 153–189, doi:10.3102/0034654307313795](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313795) (verified)
- Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015), 40 studies and 70 effect sizes on item-based feedback in computer environments: **elaborated feedback (EF) 0.49**, knowledge of correct response (KCR) 0.32, knowledge of result (KR, right or wrong) 0.05. EF was especially better for higher-order outcomes. Effects were larger in mathematics than in languages, social science or science, and delayed timing reduced effects. — [Van der Kleij et al. 2015, *RER* 85(4), 475–511, doi:10.3102/0034654314564881](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881) (verified)
- Sadler (1989): for feedback to improve work, the learner must hold a concept of the standard, compare their actual performance with it, and act to close the gap. Developing learners' evaluative judgment is the goal. — [Sadler 1989, *Instructional Science* 18(2), 119–144, doi:10.1007/BF00117714](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00117714) (verified)
- Nicol & Macfarlane-Dick (2006) reinterpret feedback research through self-regulated learning and give seven principles of good feedback practice. Students already generate internal feedback, and teaching should build that capacity, with students in a proactive rather than reactive role. — [Nicol & Macfarlane-Dick 2006, *Studies in HE* 31(2), 199–218, doi:10.1080/03075070600572090](https://doi.org/10.1080/03075070600572090) (verified)
- Nicol (2021): internal feedback through comparison (see Section 3). — [doi:10.1080/02602938.2020.1823314](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2020.1823314) (verified)
- Carless & Boud (2018): student feedback literacy has four interrelated features: **appreciating feedback, making judgments, managing affect, taking action**. Peer feedback and analysing exemplars are given as enabling activities. — [Carless & Boud 2018, *Assessment & Evaluation in HE* 43(8), 1315–1325, doi:10.1080/02602938.2018.1463354](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2018.1463354) (verified)
- Winstone, Nash, Parker & Rowntree (2017): a systematic review and taxonomy of the "recipience processes" behind learners' agentic engagement with feedback. — [Winstone et al. 2017, *Educational Psychologist* 52(1), 17–37, doi:10.1080/00461520.2016.1207538](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2016.1207538) (verified bibliographic; content not retrieved)
- Aleven, Roll, McLaren & Koedinger (2016), in intelligent tutors: feedback on *help-seeking behaviour* made students use on-demand help more deliberately, even after it was withdrawn, but **did not improve domain learning**. — [Aleven et al. 2016, *IJAIED* 26(1), 205–223, doi:10.1007/s40593-015-0089-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-015-0089-1) (verified)

### Inferences
- For a grammar or tone fixer, the evidence favours **elaborated, task-level, forward-looking** feedback ("this pattern; why; watch for it next time") over a corrected text alone, which is effectively KCR (d ≈ 0.32), or a score (KR, d ≈ 0.05).
- "Preachy" is a real risk in Kluger & DeNisi's terms. Commentary about the learner ("you always make this mistake", praise, grades) shifts attention toward the self and is where feedback backfires. Keep feedback about the text and the pattern, and keep it short.
- Language outcomes showed smaller feedback effects than mathematics (Van der Kleij), and motivational outcomes smaller than cognitive ones (Wisniewski). Expect modest gains per session; value comes from accumulation across sessions.
- Feedback-literacy framing suggests the coach should progressively hand over judgment: from "here's the fix" to "spot it yourself" to "you self-checked this pattern". That is the Sadler/Nicol endpoint.

### Gaps
- Effects of feedback timing (immediate vs delayed) remain inconsistent: Van der Kleij found delayed timing reduced effects, and Wisniewski did not model timing. I found no strong evidence specific to writing.
- These meta-analyses mostly pool school-age and non-language tasks; their transfer to adult writing support is an inference.

## 5. Learning from errors: hypercorrection, erroneous examples, productive failure

### Takeaway
Making errors and then getting corrective feedback is beneficial, not harmful. High-confidence errors are corrected especially well (hypercorrection), though they can return later. Asking learners to *find and fix errors* in examples (erroneous examples) yields delayed, not immediate, gains of about d = .33, and learners *like it less*. Attempting before instruction (productive failure) beats instruction-first at about g = 0.36, but not for young children or domain-general skills.

### Cited Findings
- Metcalfe (2017), *Annual Review of Psychology*: experiments show that errorful learning followed by corrective feedback benefits learning, particularly when people strongly believed their error was correct. — [Metcalfe 2017, *Annu. Rev. Psychol.* 68, 465–489, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022) (verified bibliographic; summary via TLDR: check)
- Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001): errors committed with high confidence are more likely to be corrected after feedback (the hypercorrection effect). — [Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001, *JEP:LMC* 27(6), 1491–1494, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491) (verified)
- Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011): the hypercorrection effect persists over a week, **but high-confidence errors return**. — [Butler et al. 2011, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 18(6), 1238–1244, doi:10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y) (verified; finding from title)
- McLaren, Adams & Mayer (2015), N = 390 middle-schoolers, decimals, web tutor: interactive **erroneous examples** (find, explain, fix) vs supported problem solving. There was no difference on the immediate post-test. The erroneous-examples group was better on a test a week later (d = .33, gain scores). The problem-solving group *liked* its intervention more (d = .21) and found the interface easier (d = .37). This replicates Adams et al. (2014). — [McLaren et al. 2015, *IJAIED* 25(4), 520–542, doi:10.1007/s40593-015-0064-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-015-0064-x) (verified)
- Adams et al. (2014): practice identifying, explaining and correcting errors helps students process problems more deeply and overcome misconceptions. — [Adams et al. 2014, *Computers in Human Behavior* 36, 401–411, doi:10.1016/j.chb.2014.03.053](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.03.053) (verified bibliographic; summary via TLDR: check)
- McLaren, van Gog et al. (2016), chemistry, two classroom experiments: **worked examples were the most efficient**, with 46–69% less study time than erroneous examples, tutored problem solving or problem solving, for similar outcomes. — [McLaren et al. 2016, *Computers in Human Behavior* 55, 87–99, doi:10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.038](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.038) (verified bibliographic; finding via TLDR: check)
- Sinha & Kapur (2021), 53 studies and 166 comparisons: problem solving followed by instruction beat instruction first, g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51]. With high fidelity to Productive Failure principles, g = 0.37–0.58; adjusted for publication bias, g = 0.87. The effect reversed (favoured instruction first) for grades 2–5 and for domain-general skills. — [Sinha & Kapur 2021, *RER* 91(5), 761–798, doi:10.3102/00346543211019105](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105) (verified)
- Keith & Frese (2008), 24 studies, N = 2,183: error management training (encouraging errors and learning from them) d = 0.44 overall, d = 0.56 for post-training transfer, and d = 0.80 for structurally different (adaptive-transfer) tasks. — [Keith & Frese 2008, *J. Applied Psych.* 93(1), 59–69, doi:10.1037/0021-9010.93.1.59](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.1.59) (verified)

### Inferences
- Users' own errors are ideal "erroneous examples" because they are personally relevant. A brief "find the issue in this sentence you wrote last week" practice item matches the find-explain-fix format that produced *delayed* gains. Expect users to rate it as less pleasant (McLaren 2015), so keep it optional and short.
- Hypercorrection suggests that a quick confidence check ("were you sure about this one?") could flag *high-confidence* errors as the most correctable. Butler et al. show they can return, so these deserve spaced re-checks in later sessions. This supports a persistent error profile with re-exposure, not one-off correction.
- The worked-example efficiency result is a counterweight: for patterns the learner has no rule for yet, a clear corrected example plus a short rule is cheaper than making them struggle. Use productive struggle only when the learner likely knows the rule (a slip, not a gap).

### Gaps
- Erroneous-example and productive-failure evidence comes from mathematics and science. I found no study of erroneous examples built from a learner's *own past writing errors*.
- I found no direct test of hypercorrection with grammar or usage errors in writing.
- The Wong & Lim (2019, *Educational Psychologist*) review of approaches to errors (prevention, permission, promotion) could not be verified: the DOI I tried did not resolve. Not cited.

## 6. AI and writing: what do AWE, Grammarly and LLM help do to learning?

### Takeaway
Automated writing evaluation improves *writing quality* (g ≈ 0.55 to 0.86 across meta-analyses), but gains are smaller for grammar than for vocabulary. The measures are mostly product scores, often on the very texts that were revised. Learners using Grammarly-style tools tend to adopt a *proofreading* stance and accept corrections without much processing. LLM feedback is comparable to or slightly below trained teachers in quality and gives similar short-term learning, with small positive effects on revision and motivation. But unrestricted GenAI help consistently raises *task performance* without raising, and sometimes while lowering, *learning*: knowledge gain, transfer, unaided performance and memory of one's own text. Effects depend on design: tutoring, guardrails and reflection preserve learning, while "automation" erodes it. AI writing help also homogenises style and lowers felt ownership.

### Cited Findings
**AWE meta-analyses and reviews**
- Ngo, Chen & Lai (2024), a three-level meta-analysis of EFL/ESL studies (1993–2021): between-group g = 0.59 (24 studies, 85 effect sizes) and within-group g = 0.98 (34 studies). AWE was **more effective for vocabulary, less for grammar**. Grammarly looked effective and Pigai did not. Medium-to-long use gave larger effects than short use. — [Ngo et al. 2024, *Interactive Learning Environments* 32(2), 727–744, doi:10.1080/10494820.2022.2096642](https://doi.org/10.1080/10494820.2022.2096642) (verified)
- Zhai & Ma (2023), 26 studies, N = 2,468 (2010–2022): g = 0.861 on writing quality. Effects were larger for post-secondary than secondary students, for EFL/ESL than native-speaker learners, and for argumentative writing. Duration, feedback combination and platform were not significant moderators. — [Zhai & Ma 2023, *J. Educ. Computing Research* 61(4), 875–900, doi:10.1177/07356331221127300](https://doi.org/10.1177/07356331221127300) (verified)
- Fleckenstein, Liebenow & Meyer (2023), a multi-level meta-analysis: g = 0.55 for automated feedback on writing performance, with significant heterogeneity and no significant subgroup differences. The retrieved abstract was garbled around the sample size ("= 2,828"). — [Fleckenstein et al. 2023, *Frontiers in AI* 6, 1162454, doi:10.3389/frai.2023.1162454](https://doi.org/10.3389/frai.2023.1162454) (verified; N: check)
- Fu, Zou, Xie & Cheng (2024), a review of 48 articles: AWE feedback improves product-oriented aspects but is "not as effective as human feedback". About 33% of studies ran under 10 weeks, 10% were single-session, and more than half had under 30 participants. Students found the feedback generic and formulaic. — [Fu et al. 2024, *CALL* 37(1–2), 179–221, doi:10.1080/09588221.2022.2033787](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2022.2033787) (verified)
- Link, Mehrzad & Rahimi (2022), two L2 classes (AWE + teacher vs teacher only): students revised teachers' lower-level feedback more often than the computer's. Students with AWE retained their accuracy improvement long-term, while those without showed lower retention. — [Link et al. 2022, *CALL* 35(4), 605–634, doi:10.1080/09588221.2020.1743323](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2020.1743323) (verified)
- Barrot (2023), a quasi-experiment with Grammarly: it improved college students' writing accuracy, which the author attributed to noticing, adaptive metalinguistic explanation and self-directed learning. Reported problems were overcorrection, cognitive overload and limited metalinguistic explanation. — [Barrot 2023, *CALL* 36(4), 584–607, doi:10.1080/09588221.2021.1936071](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2021.1936071) (verified)

**Engagement with Grammarly-type corrections**
- Koltovskaia (2020), two ESL students revising with Grammarly, using screencasts and stimulated recall. One questioned the feedback but rarely verified it; the other **over-relied on it and accepted it blindly**. Both made only moderate changes. — [Koltovskaia 2020, *Assessing Writing* 44, 100450, doi:10.1016/j.asw.2020.100450](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asw.2020.100450) (verified)
- Ranalli (2021), six Mandarin-L1 university students with Grammarly: participants adopted a **proofreading rather than a learning orientation**, "perhaps because most of it included specific corrections, thus requiring little work on the user's part". Trust in the tool, more than proficiency, shaped engagement. — [Ranalli 2021, *JSLW* 52, 100816, doi:10.1016/j.jslw.2021.100816](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2021.100816) (verified)

**LLM vs human feedback quality and short-term outcomes**
- Steiss et al. (2024), 200 human vs 200 ChatGPT feedback pieces on secondary essays, rated on five criteria. Trained humans were better on every criterion except being criteria-based: clear directions, accuracy, prioritising essential features, supportive tone. Quality did not differ by students' language status. — [Steiss et al. 2024, *Learning and Instruction* 91, 101894, doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2024.101894](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2024.101894) (verified)
- Escalante, Pack & Barrett (2023). Study 1: N = 48 university English-as-a-new-language learners, 6 weeks; GPT-4 feedback vs a human tutor showed **no difference in learning outcomes**. Study 2: N = 43; preference split nearly evenly. — [Escalante et al. 2023, *IJETHE* 20, 57, doi:10.1186/s41239-023-00425-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-023-00425-2) (verified)
- Lin & Crosthwaite (2024): teacher WCF mixes direct and indirect forms, with some inaccuracy. GPT-assisted WCF tends toward metalinguistic comments or reformulation, **varies its whole approach across runs with the same prompt and text**, and gives accurate but redundant corrections. — [Lin & Crosthwaite 2024, *System* 127, 103529, doi:10.1016/j.system.2024.103529](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2024.103529) (verified)
- Banihashem et al. (2024), 74 Dutch graduate students: ChatGPT feedback was more *descriptive*, while peers more often *identified problems*. The authors suggest the two are complementary. — [Banihashem et al. 2024, *IJETHE* 21, 23, doi:10.1186/s41239-024-00455-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-024-00455-4) (verified)
- Meyer et al. (2024), an RCT with N = 459 upper-secondary EFL students: GPT-3.5 feedback vs revising without feedback improved revision performance (d = .19, measured by automated essay scoring), task motivation (d = .36) and positive emotions (d = .34). The outcome was the revised draft, not new writing. — [Meyer et al. 2024, *Computers & Education: AI* 6, 100199, doi:10.1016/j.caeai.2023.100199](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2023.100199) (verified)

**GenAI help vs learning (performance–learning dissociation)**
- Fan et al. (2025), a randomized lab study, N = 117 university students on a writing task, comparing ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics checklist tools and no tool. The ChatGPT group improved essay scores most, but **knowledge gain and transfer did not differ**. Self-regulated learning sequences differed, and the authors warn of "metacognitive laziness". — [Fan et al. 2025, *BJET* 56(2), 489–530, doi:10.1111/bjet.13544](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544) (verified)
- Kosmyna et al. (2025, arXiv preprint; v1 10 Jun 2025, v2 31 Dec 2025), N = 54 over three sessions (LLM, search engine or brain-only) and 18 in a crossover fourth session. The LLM group showed the weakest EEG connectivity, the lowest self-reported essay ownership, and difficulty accurately quoting their own essays; essays were homogeneous within groups. It is not peer-reviewed. A formal comment raises concerns about sample size, reproducibility, EEG methods and inconsistent reporting. — [Kosmyna et al., arXiv:2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872) (verified as preprint); critique: [Stanković et al., arXiv:2601.00856](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2601.00856) (check)
- Darvishi et al. (2024), a randomized experiment with **1,625 students** across 10 courses on AI prompts that improve peer-review comments. When the AI assistance was removed, students "tended to rely on rather than learn from AI assistance". Self-monitoring checklists partly filled the gap but were less effective than AI, and AI plus checklists was no better than AI alone. — [Darvishi et al. 2024, *Computers & Education* 210, 104967, doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104967](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104967) (verified)
- Bastani et al. (2025), a field experiment with about 1,000 high-school maths students (not writing, but the clearest guardrail evidence). With access during practice, grades improved 48% with a standard ChatGPT interface ("GPT Base") and 127% with a learning-safeguarded "GPT Tutor". When access was removed, GPT Base students scored **17% lower than never-users**, and the tutor's safeguards largely mitigated that harm. — [Bastani et al. 2025, *PNAS* 122(26), e2422633122, doi:10.1073/pnas.2422633122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) (verified)
- Stadler, Bannert & Sailer (2024), N = 91: LLM users reported lower cognitive load but produced lower-quality reasoning than search-engine users. Homogeneity of recommendations did *not* differ. — [Stadler et al. 2024, *Computers in Human Behavior* 160, 108386, doi:10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386) (verified)
- Contractor & Reyes (2026, arXiv preprint), a proctored in-person randomized experiment: undergraduates studied a topic and wrote an essay with or without AI, then took unaided tests immediately and one week later. AI access raised test scores, with a smaller gain persisting a week later. Gains remained for students who used AI as a tutor ("augmentation") and **faded for those who had AI write for them ("automation")**. — [Contractor & Reyes, arXiv:2607.08849](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2607.08849) (check: preprint record seen only via OpenAlex)
- Niloy et al. (2024), N = 600 students from 10 universities: ChatGPT use was associated with worse creative-writing originality, presentation, accuracy and elaboration. The analysis (Wilcoxon) is methodologically weak. — [Niloy et al. 2024, *J. Computer Assisted Learning* 40(2), 919–930, doi:10.1111/jcal.12929](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12929) (verified bibliographic; treat the finding cautiously)

**2025–2026 RCTs and controlled studies of LLM feedback for language and writing**
- De Simone et al. (2025), World Bank Policy Research WP 11125, an RCT in Nigerian senior secondary schools: 6 weeks of after-school Microsoft Copilot (GPT-4) tutoring for English. The effect was **0.31 SD** on a combined assessment (English, AI knowledge, digital skills) and **0.23 SD on English**, equivalent to an estimated 1.5–2 years of business-as-usual schooling. Effects were largest for girls and higher-baseline students. The programme was teacher-guided tutoring, not a grammar fixer. — [De Simone et al. 2025, doi:10.1596/1813-9450-11125](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11125) (verified)
- Lu, Zhang & Li (2026, preprint): Socratic vs direct-answer AI feedback (Section 2). Delayed near-transfer OR = 1.80, plus far-transfer gains and better calibration. — [doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-10545020/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10545020/v1) (verified as preprint)
- Zhang (2025), an RCT with 259 Chinese undergraduates over 4 weeks: Qwen-powered AI feedback vs instructor feedback, analysed by difference-in-differences. The AI group improved more overall (β = 0.149), especially in organization (β = 0.311) and content (β = 0.191). Accuracy or transfer to new texts was not reported in the abstract. — [Zhang 2025, *Behavioral Sciences* 15(5), 600, doi:10.3390/bs15050600](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15050600) (verified)
- Phạm Huy (2026), a 12-week quasi-experiment with N = 90 Vietnamese low-intermediate EFL learners in three groups: AI corrective feedback; teacher metalinguistic feedback; AI CF + a structured **metacognitive reflection log**. The reflection-log group beat AI-CF-only on grammatical knowledge (error-identification test) and on the proportion of *deep* revisions, with teacher feedback in between. The author frames unreflective AI correction as an "AI Feedback Paradox". — [Phạm Huy 2026, *Research Studies in English Language Teaching and Learning* 4(4), 823–846, doi:10.62583/rseltl.v4i4.150](https://doi.org/10.62583/rseltl.v4i4.150) (verified; smaller venue, not randomized)
- Naqdi Babaee (2026), a nonrandomized study with N = 46 university EFL learners over 4 weeks and two feedback-revision cycles: ChatGPT feedback beat teacher feedback on overall writing (partial η² = .282) but **not on grammatical accuracy**. Learners noted concerns about trust and contextual fit. — [Babaee 2026, *Discover Education* 5, 1211, doi:10.1007/s44217-026-02329-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44217-026-02329-5) (verified)
- Fütterer et al. (2026), an RCT with 371 students in grades 7–9 over six 45-minute sessions in physics or English: GenAI interventions targeting utility value or cognitive strategies vs a standard ChatGPT control showed **no significant advantage** on effort, domain knowledge or elaboration-strategy use. Exploratory: more meaningful engagement went with sustained interest. — [Fütterer et al. 2026, *Educational Psychology Review* 38, 42, doi:10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10133-8) (verified)
- Kim et al. (2026), 36 Chinese graduate students: students with high AI literacy engaged GenAI collaboratively, including in metacognitive activities such as planning, while those with low AI literacy interacted less. — [Kim et al. 2026, *J. Computing in Higher Education* 38(1), 504–525, doi:10.1007/s12528-025-09444-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12528-025-09444-6) (verified)
- A three-level meta-analysis of GenAI-assisted EFL writing instruction (2023–2026), moderated by teacher mediation, is *registered but not yet reported*. — [Boonkongsaen, OSF, doi:10.17605/osf.io/c84zj](https://doi.org/10.17605/osf.io/c84zj) (check)

**Homogenisation of voice, ownership and influence**
- Agarwal, Naaman & Vashistha (2025): AI suggestions homogenised Indian participants' writing toward Western styles (Section 3). — [doi:10.1145/3706598.3713564](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713564) (verified)
- Doshi & Hauser (2024), an online experiment on short stories: access to LLM story ideas made stories rated more creative, better written and more enjoyable, especially for less creative writers, but **AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other**, an individual vs collective trade-off. — [Doshi & Hauser 2024, *Science Advances* 10(28), eadn5290, doi:10.1126/sciadv.adn5290](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290) (verified)
- Padmakumar & He (2023 arXiv; ICLR 2024): co-writing argumentative essays with InstructGPT, but not base GPT-3, significantly reduced diversity, raising similarity across authors and lowering lexical and content diversity. The reduction came from the model's contributed text; **user-written text was unaffected**. — [Padmakumar & He, arXiv:2309.05196](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2309.05196) (verified as arXiv record; ICLR venue: check)
- Draxler et al. (2024), two studies (n = 30 and n = 96), the "AI Ghostwriter Effect": users did not feel they owned AI-generated text but still did not publicly declare AI authorship. **More influence over the text increased ownership.** — [Draxler et al. 2024, *ACM TOCHI* 31(2), 1–40, doi:10.1145/3637875](https://doi.org/10.1145/3637875) (verified)
- Dhillon et al. (2024), a within-subjects field experiment with N = 131: next-*paragraph* suggestions improved quality and productivity, especially for non-regular writers, while next-*sentence* suggestions did not. Higher scaffolding brought "a moderate decrease in text ownership and satisfaction". — [Dhillon et al. 2024, CHI '24, doi:10.1145/3613904.3642134](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642134) (verified)
- Jakesch et al. (2023), N = 1,506: an opinionated LLM writing assistant shifted both the opinions in participants' posts and their later attitudes. — [Jakesch et al. 2023, CHI '23, doi:10.1145/3544548.3581196](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581196) (verified)

### Inferences
- AWE meta-analytic effects (g ≈ 0.55 to 0.86) are mostly writing-quality scores, often from within-group or short studies, so they overstate *learning*. The grammar-specific effect is weaker (Ngo). For a grammar feature, the relevant precedent is Ranalli and Koltovskaia: ready-made corrections invite click-to-accept proofreading.
- The strongest design-relevant pattern across Fan, Darvishi, Bastani and Contractor & Reyes is **performance ≠ learning**. Unrestricted "do it for me" help raises the product and can lower unaided ability. Tutor-style guardrails (Bastani's GPT Tutor), reflection logs (Phạm Huy), Socratic self-repair (Lu et al.) and "augmentation" use (Contractor & Reyes) preserve or improve learning.
- Because LLM WCF varies across runs (Lin & Crosthwaite) and is below trained teachers on accuracy and prioritisation (Steiss), a persistent error profile needs a **stable error taxonomy and consistent explanations**. Otherwise the same pattern gets explained differently each session.
- On ownership and voice: more user influence means more ownership (Draxler), and human-written text stays diverse (Padmakumar & He). So offering *localised edits the user accepts* is preferable to a full AI rewrite for both learning and voice.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed RCT yet tests an LLM grammar or tone fixer on **accuracy in new, unaided texts weeks later**, which is the outcome that matters for this feature. Most LLM-feedback studies measure the revised draft (Meyer), overall writing scores (Zhang; Babaee), or short knowledge tests (Fan).
- The Kosmyna et al. EEG findings are a preprint with a small, attrited sample (18 in session 4) and published methodological critiques; do not treat them as established.
- Contractor & Reyes (2026) and Lu et al. (2026) are preprints; their details beyond the abstracts were not checked.
- I found no study of *cross-session personal error profiles* in LLM writing tools.

## 7. The design question: how can an AI fix grammar or tone *and* teach, without being slow or preachy?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a two-layer response. First, give the user the fix fast, because they asked and comprehensive correction is legitimate for the draft. Then add a thin, optional learning layer focused on one to three *recurring, rule-governed* patterns: locate, let the user attempt, reveal with a one-line rule, compare versions for tone, and log the pattern to the profile. Bring it back as a quick check in the next session, and judge success by error rates in *new* texts. Unrestricted rewriting without that layer predicts better drafts but not better writers.

### Cited Findings
These are the strongest supports, cross-referenced from Sections 1–6:
- Fixing ≠ learning: revision gains do not predict new-text gains ([Truscott & Hsu 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2008.05.003); [Karim & Nassaji 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168818802469); [Suzuki et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2018.12.017); [Vuogan & Li 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.3178)). GenAI raises essay scores without raising knowledge or transfer ([Fan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544)).
- WCF does produce durable moderate gains in new writing ([Brown, Liu & Norouzian](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221147374); [Van Beuningen et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00674.x); [Bitchener & Knoch 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amp016)).
- Revision opportunity after correction strengthens learning ([Shintani, Ellis & Suzuki 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12029)).
- Locating is enough to trigger self-correction, and labels add little ([Ferris & Roberts 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(01)00039-X); [Chandler 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(03)00038-9)). Guided self-repair by AI improves delayed transfer and calibration (preprint: [Lu et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10545020/v1)).
- Elaborated > corrected-answer-only > right/wrong ([Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881); [Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087)). Self-focused feedback backfires ([Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254)).
- Focused feedback for learning ([Sheen et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2009.09.002); [Sheen 2007](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2007.tb00059.x)); error tracking plus self-correction ([Lalande 1982](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1982.tb06973.x)); reflection logs with AI feedback ([Phạm Huy 2026](https://doi.org/10.62583/rseltl.v4i4.150)).
- Tone: explicit pragmatics instruction > implicit ([Shakki 2022 summarising Jeon & Kaya 2006](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1016661)); comparing cases, then stating the principle ([Alfieri et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712); [Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4)); AI homogenises style ([Agarwal et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713564)).
- Guardrails prevent crutch effects ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122); [Darvishi et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104967); [Contractor & Reyes 2026, preprint](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2607.08849)).
- Delayed benefits of find-and-fix come with lower liking ([McLaren et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-015-0064-x)). High-confidence errors are corrected well but return ([Butler et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y)).
- Ownership rises with user influence ([Draxler et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3637875)), and heavy AI scaffolding lowers ownership ([Dhillon et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642134)).

### Inferences (design moves, each mapped to its evidence)
1. **Fix first, teach thin.** Return the corrected text immediately, with *changes marked*, not a silent rewrite (noticing: Schmidt; Sachs & Polio). Below it, add a "1–3 patterns worth learning" strip; the rest are silent fixes (focused vs comprehensive). This keeps speed.
2. **Pick patterns by recurrence and treatability.** Prefer patterns that recur in the profile and are rule-governed (articles, tense, agreement, prepositions in set phrases), where focused feedback plus a short rule has the best evidence (Sheen 2007; Bitchener & Knoch). Leave idiosyncratic or one-off issues as silent fixes.
3. **Locate → attempt → reveal (one tap).** For focus patterns, offer "spot it?" with the span highlighted. The user can try or tap "show". Use direct correction for grammar the user clearly cannot produce yet, and locate-only for word-choice or mechanics slips (Van Beuningen; Chandler; Ferris & Roberts; Lu et al.). Keep this optional to avoid the liking penalty (McLaren 2015).
4. **One-line "why" plus a contrasting example, never a lecture.** Use elaborated feedback (Van der Kleij), kept task-level (Kluger & DeNisi). No praise or blame about the person and no "you always…". Use neutral pattern framing ("this pattern showed up 3 times in your last 4 texts").
5. **Tone as contrasting cases.** Show the user's line against one or two alternatives on a stated axis (more direct or softer; more formal or casual) for the named reader. Ask which fits, *then* name the principle: hedging, indirect request, closing formula (Alfieri; Schwartz & Bransford; explicit pragmatics). Allow "your original is right for this reader", and adapt to the user's cultural context rather than one default norm (Agarwal; Hohenstein).
6. **Personal error profile across sessions.** Log the pattern category, an example from the user's own text, the date, whether the user self-corrected, and confidence if asked (Lalande's Error Awareness Sheet; hypercorrection). Use a fixed taxonomy so explanations stay consistent across runs (Lin & Crosthwaite).
7. **Feed forward and spaced re-checks.** At the start of the next writing task, give a single "watch for: X" cue (feed forward: Hattie & Timperley). Optionally offer a one-item find-the-error check built from the user's own past sentence (erroneous examples; error return in Butler et al.). Re-surface high-confidence errors later.
8. **Measure transfer, not fixes.** Track the per-pattern error rate in *new* texts over time as the coach's success metric, and show it as progress ("article errors: 5 → 1 per 300 words"). Avoid "errors fixed today" (Truscott & Hsu; Brown et al.).
9. **Guardrails against the crutch.** Default to localised suggestions the user accepts, not wholesale rewrites, to preserve voice and ownership and reduce homogenisation (Draxler; Padmakumar & He; Dhillon). A short optional reflection ("which of these will you watch for?") is the cheapest version of Phạm Huy's reflection log. Respect a stated "just fix it" mode (Storch & Wigglesworth on goals), but make learning mode the gentle default for users who signed up for coaching.
10. **Progressive hand-over.** As a pattern's new-text error rate drops, shift from showing the fix, to locate-only, to "self-check this pattern before I look". This builds evaluative judgment and feedback literacy (Sadler; Nicol; Carless & Boud).

### Gaps
- None of these combined designs has been tested end-to-end in an RCT measuring new-text accuracy. The design is an extrapolation from component evidence: mostly L2 English, school or university, single-feature grammar studies, and maths-based guardrail studies.
- How "learning mode" defaults affect willingness to keep using a tool (attrition) is unstudied. McLaren's lower liking and Darvishi's reliance findings suggest a real tension.
- The size of the benefit of a cross-session error profile, on its own, is unknown.

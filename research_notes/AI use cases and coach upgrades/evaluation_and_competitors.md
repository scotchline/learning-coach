## Q1. Pedagogical evaluation frameworks and benchmarks for LLM tutors, and the known problems with LLM-as-judge for pedagogy

### Takeaway
The field has settled on a small set of rubric families: Google's five learning-science principles (cognitive load, active learning, metacognition, curiosity, adapting to the learner) and MBZUAI's eight-dimension mistake-remediation taxonomy (MRBench / BEA 2025). Benchmarks are almost all math and English-only, and they measure single tutor turns, not learning. Prompted LLM judges are unreliable on the subtle pedagogical dimensions (in MRBench, some correlations with humans are negative). A small reward model trained on preference pairs does better, and human spot-checks are still needed. The 2026 large studies (Eedi/Google, OpenAI) use LLM auto-raters at scale, but only as audit tools next to human review and unassisted outcome tests.

**MBZUAI taxonomy, MRBench, BEA 2025 shared task**

  6. Coherence with the student's prior responses — Yes / To some extent / No (Yes)
  7. Tutor tone — Encouraging / Neutral / Offensive (Encouraging)
  8. Human-likeness — Yes / To some extent / No (Yes)

**LLM-as-judge for pedagogy: 2026 evidence**
- [verified] Gao, Shen, Li, "Can LLMs Judge Pedagogy? Assessing Conversational AI STEM Tutoring with AI-as-a-Judge," AIME-Con 2026 (NCME), pp. 612–621. Judges were Nemotron-3-Super-120B and GPT-OSS-120B, scoring a rubric adapted from the CPS-R Questioning and Thinking subscale. Agreement is stronger on observable features (Cognitive Demand) and weaker on interpretive ones (Encouraging Metacognition, Differentiation). Chance-corrected reliability is sensitive to skewed score distributions (a base-rate effect). The two judges differ systematically. Conclusion: "human oversight remains necessary." — [ACL Anthology 2026.aimecon-main.69](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-main.69/)
- [check] Other snippet-level claims: a 2025 AIED pilot on Socratic dialogue found LLM judges match humans on surface qualities (encouragement, actionable guidance) but not on subtle ones (recognising errors); judge reliability is low wherever human-human agreement is low; position bias. I did not open these sources. — [search result pointing to arXiv 2511.09785](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.09785v2)
- [verified] The Eedi/Google expanded RCT (Oct 2026) used Gemini Flash auto-raters to apply an eight-category codebook to revised drafts, flag maths and tone errors, check adherence to pedagogical guidelines and code 17 binary student-behaviour indicators. Humans approved every message before it reached a student. Rubric audit of delivered transcripts: the AI withheld direct answers until the student tried in 98.8% of conversations (experts 96.3%); it asked a follow-up check-for-understanding question in 91.3% (experts 60.0%). — [LearnLM/Eedi Oct 2026 PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/learnlm_oct26.pdf)
- [verified] OpenAI's planned "Learning Outcomes Measurement Suite" (Mar 2026) includes learning-interaction classifiers, "learning quality graders," longitudinal learning graders (engagement, persistence, metacognitive strategies), and standardized cognitive/metacognitive instruments. It is being validated through an RCT and is not yet released. — [OpenAI, 4 Mar 2026](https://openai.com/index/understanding-ai-and-learning-outcomes/)

### Inferences
- The MRBench eight dimensions transfer almost directly to writing and grammar feedback: mistake identification, location, revealing (here, rewriting the student's sentence), guidance, actionability, coherence, tone, human-likeness. Adding "student does the correction" and "error-pattern naming" dimensions would cover the coach's core behaviour. The 3-level label scheme with a "desired value" per dimension makes automated pass/fail tests simple.
- Treat any LLM judge as a measurement instrument that needs validation. Hand-label 50–100 coach turns on each dimension, ideally with two raters reporting Cohen's κ (MRBench's human κ ≈ 0.71 is a realistic ceiling). Then report judge–human agreement per dimension, not overall. Expect good agreement on observable dimensions (did it reveal or rewrite, is there a question, tone) and poor agreement on interpretive ones (correct diagnosis, adapting to level).
- Use binary or checklist items wherever possible ("Did the response contain a full corrected version of the student's sentence? Y/N") rather than Likert. The AIME-Con paper and MRBench both point to observable features as the reliable ones.
- Watch base-rate effects. If 95% of turns pass a dimension, κ can look poor even when the judge is fine. Report raw agreement and prevalence alongside κ.
- Use at least two different judge model families and log disagreements; the AIME-Con paper found systematic judge differences. Randomise the A/B order to limit position bias.
- None of these benchmarks measures learning. Arena-style preference and rubric scores show that the coach behaves pedagogically, not that students learn. That gap is the reason to run a real pilot (Q3).

### Gaps
- I found no published benchmark for writing or grammar tutoring dialogues with an MRBench-style taxonomy. The BEA and MRBench data are math only, English only.
- I could not open the 2025 AIED Socratic-judge pilot or the AIME-Con PDF, so I have no per-dimension agreement numbers for them.
- LearnLM-Tutor ASU deployment outcomes (Section 7 of 2407.12687) not read.
- No independent (non-vendor) replication of the Gemini arena preferences found.

## Q2. Simulated-student methods: how papers build simulated learners with misconceptions, and the known limits

### Takeaway
The standard recipe is to prompt an LLM with a persona, a target misconception or incorrect solution, and instructions to stay in character (MathDial). Newer work trains simulators or separates a latent "cognitive state" from response generation. The best-documented failure is **sycophantic capitulation**: a 2026 paper found that simulators across seven models drop their misconception after *any* corrective signal, relevant or not. That inflates tutor scores, because any feedback appears to "work." Simulated students are fine for testing behaviour (does the tutor reveal answers, does it locate the error). They are weak evidence for effectiveness and need validation against real learner data.

### Cited Findings
- [verified] MathDial's collection method: teachers converse with an LLM "prompted to act as a student with common errors," grounded in a multi-step problem plus a real incorrect solution. The goal is to avoid the privacy issues of real sessions and the low quality of crowdsourcing. — [arXiv 2305.14536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14536)
- [verified] Do, Sonkar, Sachan, "Simulating Students or Sycophantic Problem Solving? On Misconception Faithfulness of LLM Simulators," arXiv **2605.12748** (May 2026; listed as a NeurIPS 2026 poster). They propose the **Selective Flip Score (SFS)**: how much more often a simulator changes its answer after *targeted* feedback on its misconception than after *misaligned* feedback (about a different plausible misconception) or *generic* feedback ("that's wrong"). Across 7 models (4B–120B), several datasets and prompting strategies, SFS is near zero: simulators "re-solve the problem from their own knowledge" whenever corrected, a "sycophantic failure mode." SFT gave up to +0.56 SFS; RL with an SFS-aligned reward was more consistent than preference optimisation. — [arXiv 2605.12748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12748); [NeurIPS listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/149080)
- [verified] Dinucu-Jianu, Macina, Daheim, Hakimi, Gurevych, Sachan, "From Problem-Solving to Teaching Problem-Solving: Aligning LLMs with Pedagogy using Reinforcement Learning," arXiv **2505.15607** (EMNLP 2025 Main, oral). Online RL on simulated student–tutor dialogues, with controllable weighting between the student's solve accuracy and pedagogical support (a Pareto frontier). A 7B tutor trained "without human annotations" comes close to LearnLM. — [arXiv 2505.15607](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15607)
- [check] Search snippets describe the reward in that paper as the simulated student's post-dialogue solve rate plus an LLM pedagogy judge, with an answer-leakage penalty. — [arXiv 2505.15607 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15607v2)
- [check] Liu, Sonkar, Wang (arXiv **2310.02439**, 2023): GPT-4 as a "novice learner" answering with specified misconceptions and as an "expert tutor" identifying them. Tutor accuracy drops as the pool of candidate misconceptions grows. — [arXiv 2310.02439](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.02439)
- [check] PEARL (arXiv **2605.29582**, May 2026): a controllable simulator that separates latent cognitive state from response generation, to model ability levels and misconceptions, plus a generative pedagogy reward model for RL on Socratic tutors. — [arXiv 2605.29582](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29582v1)
- [check] StudentSim (arXiv **2609.01591**; NeurIPS 2026 listing): per-student simulators fitted from sparse real data (including the EFCAMDAT L2 English essay corpus), scored on behavioural fidelity and responsiveness to guidance; reportedly better than GPT-5.4 on both. — [arXiv 2609.01591](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01591)
- [check] Marquez-Carpintero, Lopez-Sellers, Cazorla, review of LLM student simulation (arXiv **2511.06078**, Nov 2025). Limits: idealised or homogenised answers, weak long-term consistency, limited emotional realism, no agreed validation criteria, Anglophone and cultural bias. — [arXiv 2511.06078](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06078v1)
- [check] "Can LLMs Effectively Simulate Human Learners?" (BEA 2025, paper 2025.bea-1.8): interviews with 12 teachers who used LLM-simulated students; design guidelines on personality, gradual knowledge building and question-asking. It also cites Käser & Alexandron (2024): only **3%** of learner-simulation studies did post-hoc validation. — [ACL Anthology preview](https://preview.aclanthology.org/acl25-workshop-ingestion/2025.bea-1.8.pdf)
- [check] Writing-specific simulation: arXiv **2507.12801** simulates a learner's errors from same-proficiency peers. Grammarly-counted errors averaged 6.16 per simulated essay vs 6.34 in real essays; a baseline produced only 0.47 errors. RILEC (arXiv **2603.07366**) generates L1-interference errors (Russian→English). A BEA 2025 Arabic study injects errors per CEFR level. — [emergentmind 2507.12801](https://api.emergentmind.com/papers/2507.12801); [arXiv 2603.07366](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07366v1)
- [verified] Google's own taxonomy rates role-played learners "Questionable validity" but "Easy to recruit" and "Always available," and real learners "Strong validity" but "Hard to recruit." It notes "simulations cannot fully capture the complexities of real-world settings." — [arXiv 2407.12687v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12687v4)

### Inferences
- **Concrete test design for the coach (writing/grammar):**
  1. Define simulated-student "cards": L1 (e.g., German, French, Italian for Swiss students), CEFR level, 2–3 *persistent* error patterns (e.g., article omission, false-friend lexis, tense sequencing), a goal (pass an exam / write a thesis), and an attitude (wants the answer fast / anxious / overconfident).
  2. Ground the errors in real learner text where possible (e.g., EFCAMDAT-style corpora or anonymised pilot texts), not random noise; the 2507.12801 result shows naive error injection under-produces errors.
  3. Add an SFS-style control to the harness: run each scenario with (a) the coach's real feedback, (b) misaligned feedback and (c) generic "that's wrong." If the simulated student "learns" equally in all three, the simulator is sycophantic and its success rates mean nothing.
  4. Script "stubbornness": the simulator must repeat the same error in a *new* sentence two turns later unless the coach's feedback names the rule and the student restates it. This tests whether the coach checks transfer, not just acceptance.
  5. Include adversarial personas ("just fix it for me," "my teacher allows it," pasting a whole essay) to test the no-rewrite policy, the most-reported competitor weakness (Q4).
- Score simulated runs mainly on **tutor-behaviour** metrics (revealing or rewriting, error location, actionability, question-per-turn, check-for-understanding rate). Do not score on "student improved," because simulator capitulation inflates that.
- For credibility, report what the 3% validation statistic says most papers skip: a small human-vs-simulator comparison, e.g., do pilot students' replies to the same coach turns look like the simulator's?

### Gaps
- I found no validated simulator for L2 writing *dialogues* (as opposed to essay generation); the closest, StudentSim, is not verified.
- I did not verify the Käser & Alexandron 3% figure in its original source.

## Q3. Pilot study designs feasible for a small team (with real university students), plus brief Swiss/EU ethics

### Takeaway
The strongest recent studies share four features a small team can copy. (1) An **unassisted post-test**, because practice performance with AI is misleading: in Bastani et al., unrestricted GPT raised practice scores 48% but lowered exam scores 17%. (2) **Transfer or near-transfer items**, as in Eedi/Google's "next unit" and delayed questions. (3) A **within-subject crossover** to get power from small N, as in Kestin et al. (Harvard). (4) **Self-report next to objective scores**, because students' perceived learning diverges from actual learning, so calibration is itself an outcome. Retention after ≥1 week is the most common gap in published work. Ethics in Switzerland: education research without health data usually falls outside the Human Research Act, so no cantonal approval is legally required. A faculty ethics committee review is advisable (journals often require it), and data protection law (FADP or cantonal law) applies.

### Cited Findings

**Reference designs**
- [verified] Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı, Mariman, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics," *PNAS* 122(26) e2422633122 (2025), DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122. Nearly 1,000 students (about 50 classes, grades 9–11) at a Turkish high school. Arms: GPT Base (ChatGPT-like GPT-4), GPT Tutor (GPT-4 with teacher-designed guardrails: hints not answers, plus teacher-supplied correct solutions and common mistakes in the prompt), and Control (books and notes). Four 90-minute sessions, each with lecture, then assisted practice (the randomised part), then a **closed-book, no-device exam** on similar problems. Practice: GPT Tutor +0.361 (+127%), GPT Base +0.137 (+48%) vs control mean 0.28. **Unassisted exam: GPT Base −0.054 (−17%); GPT Tutor −0.004 (not significant).** Mechanism: "crutch" use (asking for and copying solutions). Self-perceptions were overly optimistic: GPT Base students did not perceive that they learned less, and GPT Tutor students perceived they did significantly better without doing better. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
- [verified] Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne, Ponti, "AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: an RCT…," *Scientific Reports* 15:17458 (2025), DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6. **Crossover RCT**: 194 eligible students in Harvard PS2 (Fall 2023). Randomised by 2–3-student peer groups. Two consecutive weeks and topics (surface tension, fluid flow): one AI-tutored at home, one in-class active learning, then switched. Pre-test and post-test per lesson (A/B forms, written from learning goals by someone not involved in the lessons; 33% analysing, 41% applying). Post-test medians: 4.5 AI vs 3.5 in-class (baseline 2.75). Effect size 0.63 SD (linear regression), 0.73–1.3 SD (quantile). Median time 49 min AI vs 60 min class. 5-point Likert items: engagement 4.1 vs 3.6, motivation 3.4 vs 3.1. Tutor: GPT-4 with instructor-written step-by-step solutions in the prompt; the platform enforced the sequence of sub-parts because the prompt alone did not. Limitations: **no retention test** (immediate post-test only), ceiling effects, run by the tutor's own developers. — [PMC12179260](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- [verified] Eedi + Google LearnLM exploratory RCT, arXiv **2512.23633** (Dec 2025). 165 students in 5 UK secondary schools. LearnLM drafted messages that 17 expert tutors reviewed. Main outcome: solving **novel problems on subsequent topics** (transfer): 66.2% vs 60.7% for human tutors alone. 76.4% of drafts approved with zero or minimal edits. — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633)
- [verified] Expanded Eedi/Google RCT (Oct 2026), "Measuring the impact of AI tutoring, from near transfer to standardised tests." N = 1,179 (ages 12–15), 10 UK state schools, 10 weeks, student-level randomisation within class. Arms: standard remediation (220), AI with question context (368), AI with personalised context (371), expert tutor (220). Outcomes, all completed **without any AI or tutor help**: immediate mistake correction (43.4% control vs 56.9% / 56.5% AI vs 57.7% expert), near transfer to the next unit (50.5% vs 58.4% / 58.3% vs 59.1%), **delayed questions returning more than 7 days later** (47.6% vs 59.9% / 59.0% vs 58.9%), a midtrial quiz (59.6% vs 62.1% / 63.5% vs 61.8%), and the standardised Star Maths test (no significant effect, e.g., +0.02 SD [−0.12, +0.16]). — [LearnLM/Eedi PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/learnlm_oct26.pdf)
- [verified] OpenAI study-mode RCT (reported Mar 2026, analysis "still underway"): over 300 college students, three arms (control using Google/YouTube with AI overviews disabled; two study-mode variants counterbalanced across subjects), baseline quizzes and onboarding surveys as covariates, nominal 40-minute sessions, **intention-to-treat** analysis. Microeconomics about 15% higher relative score vs control; neuroscience directionally positive but not distinguishable. Onboarding and technical issues cut study time. OpenAI's stated lesson: measure durability over time, not one exam. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/understanding-ai-and-learning-outcomes/)
- [verified] Constructs OpenAI says it will track: Autonomous Motivation ("learners shaping their own studies vs being directed by the model"), Productive Engagement, Task Persistence, Metacognition (planning, reflecting, monitoring) and Recall. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/understanding-ai-and-learning-outcomes/)

**Calibration and metacognition**
- [verified] Fernandes, Villa, Nicholls, Haavisto, Buschek, Schmidt, Kosch, Shen, Welsch, "AI Makes You Smarter, But None The Wiser" / "Performance and Metacognition Disconnect…," arXiv **2409.16708** (journal ref. vol. 175, Feb 2026, art. 108779). 20 LSAT problems with AI. Study 1 N = 246, Study 2 N = 452. Scores were +3 vs a norm population, but participants **overestimated their performance by 4 points**. The usual Dunning-Kruger pattern "ceased to exist with AI." Higher AI literacy went with *less* accurate self-assessment. — [arXiv 2409.16708](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16708)
- [check] Journal is *Computers in Human Behavior* per search snippets. — [Aalto research portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/ai-makes-you-smarter-but-none-the-wiser-the-disconnect-between-pe/)

**Writing-specific outcomes and offloading**
- [check] Kosmyna et al., "Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task," arXiv **2506.08872** (Jun 2025): 54 participants in LLM / search / brain-only groups, with 18 completing a crossover session 4. LLM users had the weakest EEG connectivity, the lowest essay ownership, and had trouble quoting their own essays. Preprint, small N. — [arXiv 2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
- [check] Errors per 100 words is a normalised global accuracy measure used in written corrective feedback (WCF) research; e.g., Colpitts & Howard (2018, Japanese EFL) counted errors per 100 words across drafts. — [search result: pressto.amu.edu.pl](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/linpo/article/download/linpo-2018-0001/28567/70928)
- [check] WCF research separates gains on *revisions* from gains in *new pieces of writing*. Bitchener et al. (2005) note their study measured revisions only. Bitchener (2008, N = 75) reports effects persisting on a delayed post-test two months later. — [JALT Journal 43.1 review](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jj43.1-art1.pdf); [Frear, new-writing study](https://ah.lib.nccu.edu.tw/bitstream/140.119/79949/1/4.pdf)
- [check] A 2023 RELC Journal study found dynamic WCF did not beat a control on most accuracy measures. — per search snippet.
- [verified] Anthropic Education Report (8 Apr 2025): 574,740 student conversations; about 47% were "Direct" (seeking answers or content with minimal engagement). Claude's responses skewed toward Creating (39.8%) and Analyzing (30.2%), which raises offloading concerns. Limitations: early adopters only, single 18-day window, no learning measure. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-education-report-how-university-students-use-claude)

**Ethics and consent (Switzerland, brief)**
- [verified] UZH: the Human Research Act (HRA/HFG) covers research on human diseases and the structure and function of the human body; such projects need Cantonal Ethics Commission (KEK) approval. For research **outside the HRA there is no legal requirement for ethical review**; faculty ethics committees (including Arts and Social Sciences) review on request, e.g., when a funder or publisher requires it. A binding jurisdiction ruling can be requested from the KEK for a fee. UZH points to its DESAT self-assessment tool for data protection and ethics. — [UZH Research on humans](https://www.research.uzh.ch/en/procedures/research-on-humans.html)
- [check] Other universities (Fribourg, Basel) route non-HRA projects to internal committees and data-protection review; one can ask swissethics for a "clarification of responsibility." — [Uni Fribourg](https://www.unifr.ch/research/en/ethics/human-research/overview.html); [Uni Basel](https://www.unibas.ch/en/University/Administration-Services/General-Secretariat/Data-Protection/Data-protection-in-teaching-and-research.html?id=content-0)
- [check] Data protection: the revised Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP, in force since 1 Sept 2023) applies to private research; cantonal universities may fall under cantonal data protection law, while ETH/EPFL follow federal law. Consent must be informed and freely given and can be withdrawn. GDPR applies if EU residents' data are processed. — [EPFL Privacy in research](https://www.epfl.ch/campus/services/data-protection/in-practice/privacy-in-research/); [forschungsdaten.info](https://forschungsdaten.info/fdm-im-deutschsprachigen-raum/schweiz/protection-of-data-privacy-in-switzerland)

### Inferences
**A realistic pilot for about 20–40 university students over 3–4 weeks:**
- **Design:** within-subject crossover (Kestin-style). Each student does two comparable writing tasks or genres, one with the coach and one with a control (the same base model without the skill, i.e., "plain ChatGPT/Claude," or a non-AI resource such as a grammar handbook). Counterbalance order and task. A Bastani-style "base model" arm is the most informative control for a prompt-only product, because it isolates what the prompt adds.
- **Primary outcome:** error recurrence in **new, unassisted writing**. A timed, no-AI writing task 1 week later (and ideally 3–4 weeks) on a new prompt in the same genre. Measure errors per 100 words overall *and* for the student's own targeted error categories logged during coaching. Code blind to condition with two coders and report κ.
- **Secondary outcomes:**
  - Transfer: an error-detection or correction task on unseen sentences containing the targeted patterns (near transfer, like Eedi's "next unit").
  - Calibration: before each item, students rate confidence (0–100%) that their sentence or answer is correct. Compute the Brier score (mean squared difference between confidence and correctness) and a confidence–accuracy gap. Bastani and Fernandes both show AI users overestimate, so improved calibration is a defensible differentiator. (The Brier definition is standard; I did not fetch a source.)
  - Reliance and autonomy: share of turns where the student requests a full rewrite; share of fixes the student types themselves; a short autonomous-motivation item set (OpenAI names this construct; pick a validated SDT scale).
  - Usability and retention: completion rate per session; "Would you keep using this for your next assignment?" (yes/no plus a reason); actual voluntary return within 2 weeks, which is a stronger signal than stated intent.
- **Analysis:** report intention-to-treat (OpenAI) plus per-protocol results. With small N, use mixed-effects models with random intercepts for student and task. Pre-register the primary outcome to avoid forking paths.
- **Always pair self-report with objective scores.** Bastani shows perceptions can point the wrong way.
- **Ethics checklist (Switzerland):** no health data, so likely outside the HRA, but get a faculty ethics committee opinion anyway. Collect informed consent covering logging of AI chats and storage of writing samples. Make clear that participation does not affect grades. Pseudonymise and strip names from essays before any LLM-based coding. State where data are stored (vendor ToS for ChatGPT/Claude consumer accounts versus an API with no-training settings). Allow withdrawal.

### Gaps
- I found no published pilot of an AI writing coach measuring errors per 100 words in *new* writing after a delay. This looks like an open niche.
- I did not fetch a primary source on Brier-score use in educational calibration studies, or a validated autonomy scale; these need a targeted lookup.
- Swiss FADP research provisions (e.g., Art. 31 on research purposes) were not read in the statute itself.

## Q4. Competitor "learning mode" features, writing/grammar specifics, critiques, and 2026 updates

### Takeaway
Every major vendor now ships a Socratic "don't just give the answer" mode. OpenAI Study Mode and Anthropic Learning are **system-prompt or style based**. Google Guided Learning is **model-based (LearnLM, fine-tuned)**. Microsoft's Study and Learn Agent ships inside M365 Education. Khan Academy's Writing Coach is teacher-centred K-12 essay feedback. The 2026 trend is **institutional lock-in and teacher visibility** (Google Classroom locks students into Guided Learning; Microsoft adds an admin-only Study and Learn mode). Published evidence comes almost entirely from the vendors. Independent critiques focus on two things: modes are easy to switch off or bypass, and on essays they still steer content and **rewrite student paragraphs**. I found no vendor evaluation specifically of grammar or writing-error learning.

### Inferences
- **Where competitors are weakest on writing:** (1) answer-revealing in writing means rewriting the student's text, and Study Mode was caught doing exactly that; (2) no vendor publishes error-recurrence or new-writing outcomes; (3) the general modes don't keep a persistent per-student error profile (OpenAI lists progress tracking only as "exploring"; Khan's Writing Coach is assignment-bound and teacher-run).
- **Differentiation options for a prompt-only coach:**
  - A hard "never return a corrected full version of the student's sentence or paragraph" rule. Test it adversarially (Q2) and publish the pass rate, a measurable claim no competitor makes.
  - A personal **error log** the student keeps (in Claude Projects/memory, or pasted back into ChatGPT), with spaced re-checks of the same pattern in new sentences. This targets the "transfer to new writing" outcome.
  - **Calibration prompts**: ask "how sure are you this sentence is correct?" before feedback, then show the hit rate. This answers the documented overconfidence problem (Bastani; Fernandes).
  - **Open and portable**: the same pedagogy in Claude (skill) and ChatGPT (paste-in), with a public test suite (MRBench-style dimensions plus SFS-style simulator controls). Vendors' modes are closed and vendor-locked; Google's and Microsoft's 2026 moves are toward institutional lock-in and teacher monitoring, which a student-owned tool can position against (privacy, student autonomy).
- **Caveat:** prompt-only modes are "instructions, not guarantees." OpenAI admits inconsistency, and the Claude Code docs say "nothing enforces it." The coach's test suite must measure consistency across many seeds and long conversations; MathTutorBench shows long dialogs are harder.

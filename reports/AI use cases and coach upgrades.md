# Turn quick fixes into lasting skill

People mostly use ChatGPT and Claude to look things up, get practical advice and work on text they have already written. On ChatGPT, editing or critiquing the user's own text is the second-largest single use (10.6% of messages), about as large as all tutoring (10.2%). Students show the same pattern: explaining concepts, searching, brainstorming, summarising and polishing their own writing lead every recent survey, and 35–49% of students use AI to improve their own prose. The learning-coach skill deliberately steps aside from that moment, so it misses a use case as large as the one it was built for. The feedback literature says stepping aside is not neutral. A silently fixed draft teaches little, because accuracy gains while revising do not predict accuracy in the next text. A fix that is marked, explained in a line for one to three recurring, rule-governed patterns, and re-checked in later texts does produce moderate, durable gains. The strongest upgrade is therefore to build the planned "Feedback on my work" mode as a fix-first writing mode. It gives the full fix at once with the changes marked, then a two-line "receipt" naming the patterns and why. It points without fixing for graded work, or when the student opts in. It shows tone as contrasting versions for a named reader, and it keeps a writing and voice profile in the student's own log. The same receipt idea carries over to code fixes and answer checks. This requires changing the plain-task rule, the trigger description (966 of the format's 1,024 characters already used) and the paste-in prompt (1,490 of its 1,500 used). Before shipping, it should pass two tests: simulated students run with controls for the known sycophancy of simulators, and a 30–40 student crossover pilot whose primary outcome is error recurrence in new writing done without AI a week later. No study has tested this combination end to end. The case rests on component evidence, mostly from short studies of L2 English.

"Record verified, finding check" means the reference is confirmed but the result came from a summary.*

## Asking, advising and editing your own text make up most AI use

The best general-population anchor is OpenAI's classification of about 1.1 million consumer ChatGPT messages from May 2024 to June 2025 ([NBER w34255](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34255/w34255.pdf), verified). Three coarse buckets carry nearly four in five messages: practical guidance (28.3% pooled), writing (28.1%) and seeking information (21.3%). The mix is moving. Writing fell from 36% to 24% of messages between July 2024 and mid-2025, and seeking information rose from 14% to 24%. Technical help dropped from 12% to about 5% as coding moved into editors and agents. **Two thirds of writing requests work on text the user supplied.** Editing or critiquing it, translating it and summarising it add up to 18.7% of all messages, against 9.4% for writing new text. Asking (49%) outnumbers doing (40%), and asking messages get the best satisfaction ratings (same source, verified). At the level of OpenAI's 24 sub-categories, the ten largest uses are:

| 2 | **Edit or critique the user's own text** | **10.6%** | Editing is almost a quarter of M365 Copilot enterprise prompts |

The cross-checks come from Microsoft's 37.5-million-conversation consumer Copilot study, which publishes ranks rather than shares ([arXiv 2512.11879](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.11879v1), verified), and its enterprise M365 data. In that enterprise data, content refinement rose from under 22% to 28.5% of prompts in the summer of 2025 ([arXiv 2605.23958](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23958v1), verified). The third cross-check is Pew's February 2026 survey of 5,119 US adults, in which 49% use chatbots ([Pew 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/), verified). Ranks 1 to 4 hold across all of these sources. Ranks 5 to 12 sit within about 1.5 points of each other and could reorder with newer data. The design point is plain. The coach's home use case, tutoring, is matched in size by the request it currently turns away: "fix my text".

Claude.ai looks more technical. Computer and mathematical tasks were 37.2% of conversations in January 2025, peaked at 40% in March 2025, and were 34–35% from November 2025 to February 2026 ([Anthropic Economic Index, Jan 2026](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/096d94c1a91c6480806d8f24b2344c7e2a4bc666.pdf), verified; [Mar 2026](https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/4053bf3440c0c85b8852052770c5b4cf882689c3.pdf), verified). **Educational Instruction is the clearest riser. It went from about 9% to 15% and became the second-largest category, "mostly... help with coursework and review."** Arts, Design and Media, which holds copyediting and fiction, runs at about 8–10%, and its late-2025 rise came "primarily" from copyediting (same Jan 2026 source, verified). Anthropic's "learning" collaboration pattern covers asking for information or explanations. It has stayed at a fifth to a quarter of Claude.ai conversations: about 23% in January 2025, about 28% in March 2025, and 21.3% for newer users vs 24.7% for long-tenure users in February 2026 ([Mar 2026](https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/4053bf3440c0c85b8852052770c5b4cf882689c3.pdf), verified). Long-tenure users also succeed more often (73.1% vs 66.7%), which the authors partly attribute to survivorship. The mix is seasonal and shifting toward personal use. Coursework fell from 19% to 12% of conversations over the 2026 winter break, while personal use rose from 35% to 42%. Classified by output, June 2026 conversations produced explanations (17%), documents (15%) and guidance (11%) ([Anthropic, Jun 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report), verified). OpenAI's 10.2% "tutoring" and Anthropic's 21–28% "learning" use different taxonomies and cannot be compared directly.

Claude Code is a delegation surface, not a learning one. In April 2025, 79% of Claude Code conversations were automation, against 49% for coding chats on Claude.ai, and "all augmentation subtypes, including Learning, were substantially lower." Yet students, academics, personal projects and learning users made up "about half" of coding interactions on both platforms ([Anthropic, Apr 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/impact-software-development), verified from a page summary). Anthropic's June 2026 study of about 400,000 sessions breaks the work down as fixing 26%, building 25%, operating software 17%, planning or exploring 14%, analysis or prose 13%, and testing 5%. Over that period fixing fell from 33% to 19%, while writing and data analysis roughly doubled. **Sessions rated as novice reached only 15% verified success and had 19% abandonment, against 28–33% success and 5–7% abandonment for everyone else** ([Anthropic, Jun 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-expertise), verified). Novices who build with agents and then fail are a coachable gap. Claude Code users who want everything done for them are not.

The sources disagree in two places. Coding is about 4% of ChatGPT messages but a third of Claude.ai traffic, because the user bases differ. Companionship is tiny in logs (2.9% of Claude.ai conversations are affective, under 0.5% companionship or roleplay) ([Anthropic, Jun 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/how-people-use-claude-for-support-advice-and-companionship), verified), yet 10% of US adults report using chatbots for emotional support (Pew, verified). The resolution is to use logs for shares of activity and surveys for reach. Even the published literature slips here: Microsoft's Copilot paper misquotes OpenAI as saying writing was "about 77%" of conversations, when 77% is the top three buckets combined ([arXiv 2512.11879](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.11879v1), verified).

## Students explain, search, brainstorm and polish their own prose

Student surveys agree on the top five even though they word their items differently. In HEPI's December 2025 poll of 1,054 UK undergraduates, 95% use AI and 94% use it for assessed work. Explaining concepts (61%), summarising articles (49%), suggesting research ideas (40%) and structuring thoughts (39%) lead, and 37% use writing-enhancement tools ([HEPI 2026](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/HEPI-Report-199-Gen-AI-Survey-2026.pdf), verified). In the Digital Education Council's 2026 global data (n = 8,422 AI-using students), the order is searching (58%), brainstorming (54%), **refining written work (49%)**, learning new concepts (45%), drafting (43%) and summarising (43%) ([DEC 2026](https://actualidad.udla.cl/wp-content/uploads/sites/15/2026/06/6a3b9fb2f4e0472d0160d771_Digital-Education-Council-AI-in-Higher-Education-Global-Survey.pdf), verified). In the German von Garrel and Mayer survey of 4,910 students, explaining concepts reaches 66.7%, translation 48.9%, and exam preparation 37.1%, up from 12.8% in 2023 ([h_da report](https://opus4.kobv.de/opus4-h-da/frontdoor/deliver/index/docId/533/file/gpt-dokumentation-final-jvg.pdf), verified). OpenAI's own survey of 1,229 US students puts starting papers (49%), summarising (48%), brainstorming (45%), exploring topics (44%) and revising writing (44%) on top ([OpenAI 2025](https://cdn.openai.com/global-affairs/openai-edu-ai-ready-workforce.pdf), verified). Among US teens, 35% use chatbots to edit something they wrote ([Pew teens 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/), verified). Pooling these with the US and German waves gives this ranking:

The CHE figures come from [CHE WS 2025/26](https://hochschuldaten.che.de/kuenstliche-intelligenz-im-studium-die-sicht-von-studierenden-im-wintersemester-2025-26/) and the Inside Higher Ed figures from its [July 2025 flash survey](https://www.insidehighered.com/index%2Ephp/node/189916), both verified. Logs tell a different story, and the gap matters for design. Anthropic's 574,740 student conversations are 39.3% creating or improving educational content and 33.5% technical explanations or solutions, and only **2.4% translating or proofreading**. Computer science makes up 38.6% of those conversations but only 5.4% of US degrees, and about 47% of conversations are "direct" answer-seeking ([Anthropic, Apr 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-education-report-how-university-students-use-claude), verified). The 2.4% reflects Claude's technical user base. It does not show that students skip proofreading. German students mostly come with ChatGPT habits: 81.5% use free ChatGPT, 44.8% DeepL and 1.9% Claude (von Garrel, verified). **For the coach, the paste-in prompt is where a writing feature reaches most students.**

Students already bring their own work to be judged. OpenAI reports up to 70 million conversations a week spent "testing what they know." Within those conversations, 63% check answers or share work for validation, 58% show the student's own attempt, 51% ask for practice and 36% check for misconceptions. OpenAI itself cautions that it cannot yet tell productive practice from quick answer-seeking ([OpenAI, Aug 2026](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/learning-never-stops-back-to-school-report-august-2026.pdf); the student-usage notes mark this verified, the overall-usage notes could not open the page). "Is this right?" is the exam-prep twin of "fix my grammar": in both, the student hands over a product and asks for a verdict.

Students also sense the cost. In DEC 2026, 21% say it is harder to work through problems without AI, 19% notice they retain less, and **66% worry that AI makes learning "too shallow"** (81% in the US and Canada) (DEC 2026, verified). In HEPI 2026, 20% name "I will learn more if I do not use AI" as a reason to hold back, behind fear of being accused of cheating (42%) and hallucinations (35%). The AI skills students most want are fact-checking AI output (51%) and knowing when and when not to use AI (45%) (DEC 2026, verified). One gap matters for the leading example: no survey asks about tone or style feedback as its own item. "Refining written work" and "revising writing" are the closest.

## A silent fix teaches little; a marked, explained, rechecked fix transfers

### Correction works, but revision gains are not learning

Grammar correction does teach. A Bayesian meta-analysis of 52 control-group studies finds **"robust evidence of the durability of moderate effectiveness"** of written corrective feedback over time, with direct, indirect and metalinguistic feedback giving similar effects ([Brown, Liu & Norouzian](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221147374), verified). Van Beuningen and colleagues followed 268 Dutch secondary-school learners into new texts written one and four weeks later. Only direct correction improved grammatical accuracy in new writing, while indirect feedback helped most with non-grammatical errors, and correction did not make students simplify their writing ([Van Beuningen et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00674.x), verified). Bitchener and Knoch tracked learners for ten months, and all three correction treatments beat the control on every post-test ([Bitchener & Knoch 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amp016), verified).

The same literature warns against judging a tool by the draft it returns. Error reduction during revision did not predict accuracy in later new writing ([Truscott & Hsu 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2008.05.003), record verified, finding check). In a three-week study, gains in revision were significant while gains in new writing were "largely non-significant" ([Karim & Nassaji 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168818802469), verified). Peer feedback also shows larger effects on revisions than on new compositions ([Vuogan & Li 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.3178), verified). Explanation also has to be attached to the student's own text. In school writing, traditional grammar lessons detached from students' writing gave d = −0.32 on writing quality ([Graham & Perin 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445), verified). **A coach that counts "errors fixed today" will overestimate learning. The meaningful number is how often a pattern comes back in the next text.**

### Where the learner looks matters more than how much the coach says

Which kind of feedback helps follows a clear pattern. Elaborated feedback (d = 0.49) beats being shown the correct response (0.32), and bare right or wrong verdicts barely work (0.05) ([Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881), verified). A corrected text with no comment is the second kind. More than a third of feedback interventions in Kluger and DeNisi's 607 effect sizes made performance worse, especially when the feedback drew attention to the self rather than the task ([Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254), verified). That is the empirical case against "you always make this mistake." A full native-like rewrite was the weakest condition for noticing: error correction beat reformulation in both of Sachs and Polio's experiments ([Sachs & Polio 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263107070039), verified). Underlining an error was about as good as labelling it ([Ferris & Roberts 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1060-3743(01)00039-X), record verified, finding check). Focused feedback on a few categories helps learning more than correcting everything ([Sheen, Wright & Moldawa 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2009.09.002), record verified, finding check). A short rule explanation kept learners ahead on delayed tests for a single rule-governed feature ([Sheen 2007](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2007.tb00059.x), verified), though it added nothing over direct correction in the ten-month study above. Giving learners the chance to rewrite after correction strengthened the effect ([Shintani, Ellis & Suzuki 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12029), verified). The first AI-specific RCT on locating versus fixing is a preprint with 70 learners of English articles. Socratic AI feedback that guided self-repair beat direct answers on delayed near transfer (OR = 1.80) and on far-transfer production, and it produced much less overconfidence, at the cost of higher cognitive load ([Lu, Zhang & Li 2026](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10545020/v1), preprint, record verified).

Today's default tools invite the wrong stance. Students using Grammarly took **"a proofreading rather than a learning orientation,"** because ready-made corrections needed little work ([Ranalli 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jslw.2021.100816), verified), and one of two students in a screencast study accepted corrections blindly ([Koltovskaia 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asw.2020.100450), verified). The hopeful counterpoint is the strongest finding for the leading example. In pre-registered experiments on cover letters, practising with an AI tool that rewrote the participant's own letter improved later writing without AI more than practising alone, googling examples or getting a human editor's feedback. **Simply viewing the AI-revised version of one's own letter helped as much as practising with the tool** ([Lira et al., "Coach not crutch"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02880), preprint, arXiv verified; sample sizes and test delay check). Put together with Sachs and Polio, the lesson is that a fix the user can see teaches as a personalised worked example, while a silent rewrite does not.

### Performance with AI rises while learning without it stalls

Studies that remove the tool afterwards show the same split. With ChatGPT, students' essay scores improved the most, yet their knowledge gain and transfer did not differ from the other groups' ([Fan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544), verified). In a randomised experiment with 1,625 students, students "tended to rely on rather than learn from AI assistance" ([Darvishi et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104967), verified). In Turkish high-school maths, unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice scores by 48% and lowered the later unassisted exam by 17%, while a hint-only tutor largely avoided the harm ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), verified). In a Python lab study, asking the AI for explanations raised understanding (+0.249) and asking for solutions lowered it (−0.312). Students with access also *felt* they had learned more when they had not ([Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09047v2), preprint, arXiv verified). Two writing results point to the remedy. GPT feedback on drafts improved revisions slightly (d = .19) and motivation more (d = .36) ([Meyer et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2023.100199), verified). Adding a structured reflection log to AI correction beat AI correction alone on grammatical knowledge and on the share of deep revisions, though that study was not randomised ([Phạm Huy 2026](https://doi.org/10.62583/rseltl.v4i4.150), verified).

### Tone is teachable, but AI quietly flattens voice

Pragmatics is among the more teachable parts of language. Explicit instruction gave d = 0.70 against controls, and implicit exposure 0.44 ([Shakki 2022 summarising Jeon & Kaya](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1016661); Shakki verified, Jeon & Kaya details check). Among 322 Swiss eighth and ninth graders writing request emails, formulaic elements such as "Dear Ms …" improved fastest, while freer interpersonal moves developed gradually and with backsliding ([Keller et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.2478/ristal-2025-0005), verified). Comparing contrasting cases beats studying cases one at a time (d = .50), especially when learners look for similarities and the principle comes after the comparison ([Alfieri et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712), verified; [Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4), verified). The risk is homogenisation. AI suggestions led Indian participants to adopt Western writing styles ([Agarwal, Naaman & Vashistha 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713564), verified). AI smart replies made messages more positive, and people suspected of using them were rated more negatively ([Hohenstein et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-30938-9), verified). **Users feel they own a text more when they had more influence over it** ([Draxler et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3637875), verified), and heavier scaffolding lowered ownership ([Dhillon et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642134), verified). Tone feedback should therefore offer choices for a named reader and be able to say "yours is fine". It should not push every text toward one "professional" norm.

### A student's own errors make the best practice items

Errors made with high confidence are corrected especially well once feedback arrives ([Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491), verified), but they tend to return later ([Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y), verified). Finding and fixing errors in examples gave delayed gains of d = .33 that were not visible immediately, and learners liked it less ([McLaren, Adams & Mayer 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-015-0064-x), verified). The closest precedent for a personal error profile is Lalande's 1982 "Error Awareness Sheet" for learners of German, bundled with coded self-correction ([Lalande 1982](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1982.tb06973.x), record verified, results check). It is old, and no study has isolated the effect of a log on its own. That gap is the main reason the pilot below measures the profile's effect directly.

## Twelve upgrades, led by a two-line receipt on every fix

One design idea runs through all twelve: **fix first, teach thin, remember in the student's file.** The person gets the result they asked for in the first reply. The learning layer is a few lines they can ignore, and continuity lives in the student-kept log, not in platform memory. That keeps the v0.1 rule of no memory except the log, and it keeps one question per turn. What changes is the rule that plain fixes of the student's own text get no learning content at all.

### The writing moment: five changes that build "Feedback on my work"

**P1. The fix receipt.** When someone asks to fix, proofread or smooth their own text and the work is not graded, the coach does exactly what was asked in the first reply: the whole text, corrected, with the changed words in bold. Below it, in at most two lines, it names the one to three patterns that recur or follow a rule, each with a short reason, and ends with one offer that needs no answer. One-off slips are fixed silently. A Swiss student writes "Can you fix my English? It's not my first language. *Dear Prof. Keller, I write you because I have a question to the exam. Since two weeks I try to understand exercise 3.*" The reply:

> Dear Prof. Keller, I **am writing to** you because I have a question **about** the exam. **For** two weeks I **have been trying** to understand exercise 3.
>
> Two patterns did most of the work: something still going on now takes *have been + -ing*, with *for* + a length of time (German *seit* misleads here), and *Frage zu* is *question about*. Save them to your writing profile?

This follows the evidence above. The changes are visible rather than silently rewritten (Sachs & Polio; Lira). The reason is elaborated rather than a bare correction (Van der Kleij). Only a few patterns are explained, attached to the student's own text (Sheen; Graham & Perin). The framing stays on the pattern, not the person (Kluger & DeNisi). If the student says "just fix it", receipts stop for the rest of the session, as the existing "just tell me" rule does for answers.

This is a deliberate change to three rules. The core loop's "Plain task (email, table)? Just do it." and the step-aside section's "do not mention coaching, quizzes or learning" both need an exception for fixes of the student's own text. In `modes.md`, "Do not rewrite passages for them" stays for graded work and for drafts brought for feedback, but a fix request on ungraded text gets the fix, because a result is what was asked for. Speed is protected because nothing waits on a reply. Preachiness is protected by the two-line cap and the opt-out.

The trigger is the harder choice, and there are two routes. The conservative route, recommended for v0.2, extends the description so the skill loads on a fix of the student's own text only when there is a learning signal: they say they are learning the language, want to improve or keep making a mistake, the text is for a course, or they paste a writing profile. "Proofread my LinkedIn post" stays closed, as the trigger tests now require. The description has 58 characters of room, so some existing wording has to be cut. The bold route, a v0.3 experiment, ships a tiny companion skill in the same repository, `fix-receipt`, that triggers on every fix of the user's own text and does only P1 and the profile save. That leaves the learning coach's trigger untouched and makes installing the companion the opt-in. The pilot should decide between them. The paste-in prompt is at 1,490 of 1,500 characters. A line such as "Their own text: fix all, bold changes, name 1-3 recurring patterns, a line each." costs about 80 characters, so another line has to shrink. It is worth the trade, because most DACH students reach the coach through ChatGPT.

**P2. Spot it first.** For graded work, and for anyone who says they want to get better, the coach does not hand back corrected sentences for the focus patterns. It points: "Sentences 2 and 4 share one tense problem. Want to try, or shall I show you?" The student fixes, and the coach confirms or shows the answer. A simple test chooses between pointing and correcting. If the student gets the pattern right elsewhere in the same text, it is a slip, so point. If they never get it right, it is a gap, so correct it directly with a one-line rule. Van Beuningen found direct correction was what moved grammar in new writing, and worked examples are the efficient route for learners who lack the rule ([McLaren et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.038), record verified, finding check). Pointing is supported by Ferris and Roberts, by the self-repair preprint (Lu et al.) and by the benefit of a revision opportunity (Shintani et al.). Mediation-based feedback with graduated hints beat direct correction on accuracy in one quasi-experiment, though rhetoric gains regressed ([Özturan & Uysal 2025](https://doi.org/10.21153/tesol2025vol34no2art2211), verified). For graded work this is the existing graded-work rule made concrete, so no rule changes. The rule may be explained, because it is method; a corrected version of the student's sentence is never given. Outside graded work it stays optional, because find-and-fix is liked less (McLaren 2015). This is also where the coach can beat competitors outright. On an argument essay, ChatGPT's Study Mode critiqued a student's paragraph "and then produced a rewrite students could copy" ([Edutopia, Feb 2026](https://www.edutopia.org/article/putting-chatgpts-study-mode-through-its-paces/), verified).

**P3. Tone as contrasting versions.** "Does this sound OK?" gets a fast answer by default: a one-line verdict, one recommended version, a one-line reason, and when the original is fine. For "I need your feedback by Friday" sent to a supervisor, that is: "Reads as an instruction to someone senior. Try: 'Could you send me your feedback by Friday? That would let me submit on time.' *Could* plus a reason sounds cooperative; if you two are on first-name terms and she likes it short, yours is fine." When the student wants to get better at tone, the coach switches to learning mode. It shows two or three versions along one named axis (softer to more direct, or more formal to more casual) for the named reader, asks which fits, and names the principle only after the pick. That order is the moderator favoured by Alfieri and by Schwartz and Bransford, and the pick counts as the turn's one question. If the reader or the cultural setting is unclear, asking about it is the question. The design answers the homogenisation findings (Agarwal; Hohenstein) by tying advice to a stated reader, and it answers Draxler by leaving the choice with the writer. It fits the existing rules and extends "mirror their register" into a stored voice (P4).

**P4. A writing and voice profile in the student's own log.** `assets/learning-log.md` gets a new section that is updated in place rather than appended, kept to about ten lines:

| Pattern | My example (date) | Rule in my words | Seen in new texts | Caught it myself? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tense with *since/for* | "Since two weeks I try" (12 Nov) | still going on → *have been + -ing*; *for* + length | 3 of last 4 | once |
| *Frage zu* | "a question to the exam" (12 Nov) | *question about* | 1 of last 4 | no |

A voice line follows: who they write to, their register, phrases to keep and things never to add ("no 'I hope this finds you well', no exclamation marks"). At most five patterns are active at once, to keep the feedback focused. A pattern earns a row only when it recurs or the student asks for one, and it is retired after three new texts without it (a proposed threshold, to be tested). When the profile is pasted at the start of a writing task, the coach gives one feed-forward line ("Watch for: *since/for* tenses") ([Hattie & Timperley 2007](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487), verified). After the draft, it reports recurrence in a line ("tenses: 1 this time, 3 last time"). Now and then it offers one find-the-error item built from the student's own earlier sentence, starting with errors they were sure about, because those are the most correctable and the likeliest to return (Butterfield & Metcalfe; Butler et al.). The progress shown is recurrence in new texts, never fixes made (Truscott & Hsu; Brown et al.). The proposal keeps the v0.1 rule of no memory except a student-kept log. It changes the log format and needs one new evidence row. It also differentiates the coach. OpenAI lists progress tracking only as planned ([OpenAI Study Mode](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-study-mode/), verified), while Google Classroom now shows teachers full chat transcripts of locked-in students ([Google Workspace Updates, Nov 2026](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/11/educators-can-now-add-guided-learning-to-assignments-in-Google-Classroom-to-help-students-get-extra-support.html), verified). A portable profile that the student owns is the opposite position.

**P5. A fixed pattern taxonomy.** A new `references/writing-patterns.md` lists about twelve categories: tense and aspect, articles, prepositions and set phrases, agreement, word order, L1 transfer and false friends, collocation, sentence boundaries, punctuation, spelling, register and politeness, hedging and directness. Each comes with a one-line rule template, an example, and guidance on when to point and when to correct. It also covers interference patterns typical of German, French and Italian speakers. The reason is consistency. LLM corrective feedback "varies its whole approach across runs with the same prompt and text" ([Lin & Crosthwaite 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2024.103529), verified), and trained teachers beat ChatGPT on accuracy and on prioritising the essential points ([Steiss et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2024.101894), verified). A profile only works if next week's session names the pattern the same way. This is a design file with no learning source; its nearest principle is 3 (self-monitoring across sessions).

### The same move across the other top use cases

**P6. The coach labels its own confidence.** This extends the verification nudge. When the coach states something the student will rely on, it tags the claim with the student's own scale (sure, fairly sure, check this) and names where to check. When the student is about to cite a source, it asks them to open it and quote the line. In LLM-based search, colour-coded confidence improved error detection and decision accuracy ([Spatharioti et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3714082), verified; magnitude check). LLM-written fact checks did not improve, and could reduce, people's ability to tell true headlines from false ones ([DeVerna et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2322823121), verified; mechanism check). Learning a topic through LLM summaries left knowledge shallower than web search, even with the same facts and with links ([Melumad & Yun 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf316), verified). GPT-4o fabricated 19.9% of citations in simulated literature reviews ([EurekAlert on JMIR](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1106130), check). Fact-checking is also the AI skill students most want (DEC, 51%). The known risk is that the model's own confidence can be miscalibrated, so the test suite seeds known-false claims (scenario 15). The rule change is from "every so often" to "every load-bearing claim gets a short label". The label must stay at a few words, or it turns into the disclaimer clutter the tone rules forbid.

**P7. "Is this right?" plants a variant for later.** The check sequence already asks for confidence before the verdict. The upgrade is that after a miss, the coach writes a variant into the review queue, due in one to two days: the same idea in a new case. This is the "returning question" mechanic that, in a 1,179-student RCT, raised accuracy on questions returning more than seven days later to 59–60%, against 47.6% for standard remediation ([Eedi & Google, Oct 2026](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/learnlm_oct26.pdf), verified). The same trial is a warning, though. Tutored students covered fewer units (median 99 vs 132) and showed no effect on a standardised test. So when an exam is close, the coach tutors only the sure-but-wrong and repeated errors and marks the rest in a line. That is the writing mode's "teach thin" applied to answers. It fits exam-prep ordering and the review queue with a one-line addition.

**P8. A learner's bug gets a guess first.** When someone says they are learning to code and asks why their code fails, the coach asks one question first ("What do you think is wrong? A guess is fine." or "What will line 6 print?"). Then it gives the fix, then a receipt naming the bug pattern. "I just need my script to run" stays a plain task, as the trigger tests require. In Anthropic's RCT with 52 mostly junior engineers, the AI-assisted group scored 50% vs 67% on a later quiz without AI (d = 0.738), and debugging showed the largest gap. "Iterative AI debugging" was the worst-scoring pattern at about 24%. Asking why after the code was generated scored about 86%, but only two people used that pattern ([Shen & Tamkin 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills), preprint, publisher page verified). The "Lead-and-Reveal" and "Trace-and-Predict" friction techniques balanced learning and effort best ([Kazemitabaar et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3708359.3712104), verified; statistics check). For Claude Code, the same behaviour can ship as an output style in a plugin. Claude Code's built-in Learning style already leaves `TODO(human)` markers at real design decisions, but output styles are instructions only, and "nothing enforces it" ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles), verified). This adds a new surface and fits the existing attempt-first rule.

**P9. Summaries as a diff; brainstorms start with one idea of the student's own.** The existing rule ("their summary before yours") now has stronger support: Melumad and Yun's seven experiments with more than 10,000 participants, and a 405-student school RCT in which note-taking beat LLM-only support on comprehension and three-day retention, even though students preferred the LLM ([Kreijkes et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2025.105514), verified; effect sizes check). The upgrade has two parts. The coach's contribution comes back as a diff against the student's version ("missing: X; misread: Y") rather than a full summary. Before a long reading, it offers two or three prequestions ([Pan & Carpenter 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5), verified; moderators check). For brainstorming in a learning or graded context, the coach asks for one idea of the student's own, of any quality, then offers short AI options as contrast. The evidence here is mixed. Coach-style hints *lowered* later unassisted performance by 10.6 points on a remote-associates test ([Kumar et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03703v2), verified), and AI ideas made stories more similar to each other ([Doshi & Hauser 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290), verified). So the brainstorming line ships only in the skill and only after scenario testing.

### Three cross-cutting bets

**P10. A calibration tally in the log.** The log gains one line: "Sure answers: 7 of 9 right this week," or for writing, "Sentences you were sure of: 9 of 10 correct." With AI, LSAT-task participants scored 3 points above a norm population but overestimated their performance by 4 points. The usual Dunning-Kruger pattern disappeared, and higher AI literacy went with *less* accurate self-assessment ([Fernandes et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16708), verified). In Bastani's trial, tutor-arm students believed they did better without doing better (verified). The self-repair preprint (Lu et al.) cut overconfidence. The tally stays about answers, not the person, as Kluger and DeNisi require. It extends the confidence ratings and the log without a new principle.

**P11. An AI-use note for graded work.** At the end of a graded-work session, the coach offers once a factual sentence or two that the student can edit to meet their course's rules: "Used an AI coach to explain the SWOT method on IKEA and to comment on my draft; all text is mine." Fear of being accused of cheating is students' top deterrent at 42% (HEPI 2026), and 76% of Fribourg and HES-SO students want clear rules on AI in exams and theses ([Unifr, Dec 2025](https://www.unifr.ch/news/de/33969/ki-tools-sind-an-hochschulen-nicht-mehr-wegzudenken), verified). No learning source supports this; it is a design decision under principle 3. It changes the rule against "commentary on AI use, study habits or integrity" by allowing one neutral offer.

**P12. Step out of the loop for distress.** If a student signals distress beyond ordinary exam stress, the coach drops the loop. It replies briefly and warmly, suggests talking to a person (a friend, student counselling), and offers to pick the studying up later. 15% of UK undergraduates use AI for friendship, company or advice (HEPI 2026, verified), as do 12% of US teens for emotional support (Pew, verified). Heavier voluntary chatbot use went with more loneliness and emotional dependence, though only correlationally ([Fang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17473v2), preprint, verified). In one pilot, procrastination fell after sessions with a human agent but not after sessions with ChatGPT ([Hennemann et al. 2025](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5281283), check). This is safety design with no learning source. It fits "match their energy".

## Prove it twice: sceptical simulated students, then a 30-student crossover

### The automated harness has to distrust its own students

The current results come from one or two runs per scenario, with a second model playing the student ([`tests/results.md`](../tests/results.md)). That shows the coach follows its rules, which the file states honestly. It is too thin for the new modes. The most important correction comes from a 2026 study across seven simulator models, whose "Selective Flip Score" was near zero: simulated students drop their misconception after *any* corrective signal, relevant or not ([Do, Sonkar & Sachan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12748), verified). A simulated student who "learns" from the coach therefore proves nothing. So the harness should do six things.

First, run every writing scenario three times: with the coach's real feedback, with misaligned feedback, and with a generic "that's wrong". "Student improved" only counts if it beats both controls. Second, script stubbornness. The simulated student repeats a logged error in a new sentence two turns later unless the coach named the rule and the student restated it. That tests whether the coach checks transfer or only acceptance. Third, ground the simulated students' errors in real learner text: L1 German, French or Italian; a CEFR level; two or three persistent patterns; a goal; and an attitude such as hurried, anxious or overconfident. Naive error injection badly under-produces errors: 0.47 per essay against 6.34 in real essays ([arXiv 2507.12801 summary](https://api.emergentmind.com/papers/2507.12801), check). Fourth, score tutor behaviour, not learning, using binary checklist items adapted from MRBench's eight dimensions: mistake identified, mistake located, answer revealed (here, sentence rewritten), guidance, actionability, coherence, tone, human-likeness ([Maurya et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09416v2), verified). Prompted LLM judges are unreliable on interpretive dimensions. In MRBench, critic correlations with humans were mostly negative. In MathTutorBench, prompted judges stayed below 0.7 accuracy ([Macina et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18940v2), verified). A 2026 study concluded that "human oversight remains necessary" ([Gao, Shen & Li 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-main.69/), verified). So the harness should use two judge model families with the order of responses randomised, hand-label about 60 coach turns, and report per-item agreement with prevalence next to κ. The human-human κ of 0.71 in MRBench is a realistic ceiling. Fifth, run each scenario on at least five seeds and report pass rates, because prompt-only learning modes are "instructions, not guarantees" and long dialogues are harder. Sixth, extend the 20-prompt trigger set with fix, proofread and tone prompts, with and without learning signals, three runs each.

### A within-student crossover can measure what matters, if it measures new texts

The pilot should copy what the strongest studies share. Kestin's Harvard RCT used a within-student crossover to get power from 194 students, but it had no retention test ([Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/), verified). Bastani's "base model" arm isolates what guardrails add, and unassisted post-tests catch the gap between practice and learning ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), verified). The proposed pilot has 30–40 non-technical students at a Swiss university who write in English as a second language, and runs four to six weeks. In week 0 they write a timed baseline text without AI in two genres, an email to a lecturer and a 250-word argumentative paragraph. Two blind coders tag every error with the P5 taxonomy and report κ, which fixes each student's three most frequent categories before any coaching. In weeks 1 and 2, each student writes at least three real texts per genre: one genre with the coach (the skill, or the paste-in prompt in their usual assistant) and the other with the same assistant without it. Genre and order are counterbalanced. In week 3, both genres are written again on new prompts, timed and without AI, followed by an error-detection test on unseen sentences built around each student's baseline categories. A repeat in week 6 is optional.

**The primary outcome is errors per 100 words in each student's baseline categories, in the new texts written without AI.** Defining the categories at baseline, and not from the coach's log, keeps the two arms comparable. The secondary outcomes cover six things. Near transfer is the error-detection score. Calibration is a confidence rating given before each detection item, scored with the Brier score. Reliance is the share of requests for a full rewrite and the share of fixes the student typed themselves, both taken from chat logs. Ownership is a single "this text sounds like me" item, in the spirit of Draxler. Friction is captured by two items, "the extra lines slowed me down" and "felt preachy", plus the opt-in rate to saving a pattern. Retention is voluntary return within two weeks after the pilot, which is a stronger signal than stated intent. The analysis should report intention-to-treat and per-protocol results, as OpenAI's study-mode trial does ([OpenAI, Mar 2026](https://openai.com/index/understanding-ai-and-learning-outcomes/); verified in one note, check in another). Use mixed-effects models with random intercepts for student and fixed effects for genre and order, and pre-register the primary outcome. Self-report always sits beside objective scores, because perceived and actual learning diverge (Lehmann; Bastani).

A crossover of about 34 students has 80% power to detect a within-student effect of about d = 0.5 at α = .05. That figure is the author's standard paired-design calculation, not a source. The pilot can therefore detect a moderate effect and estimate its size, but it cannot prove a small one. Spillover cuts against finding an effect: a student who learns a pattern in the coached genre can carry it into the other, which biases toward null. Run the pilot with that bias in mind and interview about eight students afterwards. Ethics are light but real. Education research without health data generally falls outside the Swiss Human Research Act, so no cantonal approval is legally required, though a faculty ethics opinion is advisable because journals ask for one ([UZH](https://www.research.uzh.ch/en/procedures/research-on-humans.html), verified). Data protection under the federal or cantonal law still applies ([EPFL](https://www.epfl.ch/campus/services/data-protection/in-practice/privacy-in-research/), check). Consent must cover chat logs and writing samples. Participation must not affect grades. Texts are pseudonymised before any LLM coding, and the study uses API access with no-training settings.

Decision rules should be fixed before the data arrive. The following thresholds are proposed, and the author should set the final ones. Ship P1 and P2 inside the skill if three conditions hold. The fix-first and receipt-length checks pass in at least 95% of seeded runs. Fewer than one in five pilot students rate the extra lines as slowing them down or preachy. The primary outcome points the right way, with a confidence interval that rules out moderate harm. Build the bold `fix-receipt` companion only if more than half of students save at least one pattern and voluntary return is no lower than for the plain assistant. Keep the brainstorming line in P9 out of the shipped skill until it has its own small test: an unassisted idea task after AI-assisted practice, as in Kumar's design. Kumar's result shows that even a "coach-style" hint can backfire, and the writing pilot cannot detect that.

## Conclusion

The step-aside rule assumed that teaching at the moment of a fix costs speed. The evidence says the useful part costs almost nothing: changes the student can see, two lines on the patterns that recur, and an offer that needs no reply. Everything that does cost speed (questions before the fix, lessons on grammar in the abstract, comments about the person) is also what the research shows to be useless or harmful. The deeper change is in what the coach measures. Revision gains mislead, so the only honest success signal for a writing coach is how often a pattern returns in the next text. That signal is visible only through a profile that travels with the student. In the writing mode, the learning log stops being an end-of-session ritual and becomes both the coach's memory and its outcome measure.

There is also an open lane. Vendor learning modes are toggles that students can switch off. On essays they have been caught rewriting student paragraphs, none publishes error recurrence in new writing, and the 2026 trend is institutional lock-in with teachers reading transcripts. An open, student-owned coach can make two public, measurable claims that none of them make: a no-rewrite pass rate on graded work under adversarial simulated students run with sycophancy controls, and error recurrence in new writing done without AI in a real pilot. Neither claim can be made today, and the pilot is what turns the design from a plausible extrapolation into evidence.

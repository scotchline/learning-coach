# Hint ladder

Read this when you need wording for a rung, when a student is still stuck after rung 4, or when the subject is not a calculation.

The ladder exists so that every piece of help is the smallest one that gets the student moving again. Each rung leaves a little less of the thinking to them, so climb slowly and come back down as soon as you can.

## The four rungs

| Rung | What you give | What stays with the student |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Guiding question | A question that points at where to look | Finding the idea and applying it |
| 2. Hint | The relevant idea, rule or definition, without applying it | Applying it to this case |
| 3. Partial step | One step done, the next one handed back | Every remaining step |
| 4. Worked example, then a similar problem | A full solution to a similar problem, then a new one of the same kind | Solving the new one alone |

Rung 4 has two halves, and the second half is the one that matters. A worked example on its own is an answer. Following it with a similar problem that the student solves alone is what turns it back into practice.

## Wording by subject type

**Calculation** (the student cannot work out a break-even point)

1. "What has to be equal at the break-even point?"
2. "Break-even is where total revenue equals total cost. Which of your numbers belong on which side?"
3. "Revenue is 40 times the quantity. You write the cost side."
4. "Here's one with different numbers: fixed cost 6,000, price 30, variable cost 18 ... so 500 units. Now yours: fixed cost 9,000, price 40, variable cost 25. Go."

**Concept** (the student cannot say what a p-value means)

1. "If there were really no effect, how surprised would you be by this result?"
2. "A p-value is about the data, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It says nothing directly about whether the hypothesis is true. Try again with that."
3. "Start your sentence with 'If the null hypothesis were true, the probability of ...' and finish it."
4. "For a coin: p = 0.03 means that if the coin were fair, a result at least this lopsided would turn up about 3 times in 100. Now say the same for the drug trial in your notes."

**Writing or argument** (the student has no thesis for an essay on remote work)

1. "After everything you've read, what do you actually think, and who would disagree?"
2. "A thesis is a claim someone could argue against, plus your main reason. 'Remote work has pros and cons' is a topic. What's your claim?"
3. "Your claim so far: remote work helps focus. Add the 'because', and one condition under which it would not hold."
4. "Example on a different topic: 'Open-plan offices reduce collaboration, because people shield themselves with headphones.' Notice claim plus reason. Now write yours for remote work."

For graded writing, rungs 3 and 4 always use a different topic from the assignment, as in the example above.

## Moving up and down

- **Up one rung at a time, after a real try.** "I don't know" is not yet a try. Ask for a guess, for the part they do know, or for what they would do first.
- **Wait.** Give the student a turn to respond to each rung before adding more. Two rungs in one message is the usual way a coach ends up giving the answer.
- **Down again quickly.** Once they are moving, the next piece of help starts at rung 1 again, or there is none.
- **Fade across the session.** If rung 2 was needed on the first problem, try rung 1 on the second and nothing on the third. Say it when you do: "This time no hints from me."

## Still stuck after rung 4

Do not run the ladder a second time on the same problem. The gap is almost always one level down: a prerequisite idea is missing. Ask what the last step was that made sense, find the missing piece, and run a short loop on that piece. Then come back.

If the session is going nowhere, say so plainly and suggest a smaller target for today. That is a planning decision, and it is theirs to make.

## Special cases

- **Graded work.** On the assigned problem you may ask guiding questions about method (which category, why, what evidence would show it) and name the general idea or rule. Neither may carry content for the answer. Partial steps and worked examples use a different case. Never finish the assigned problem and never add points to it.
- **"Just tell me."** Skip the ladder and give the answer with the reasoning visible, as the standing rule says. Afterwards you can offer the second half of rung 4: "Want to try one like it on your own?"
- **Right answer, no idea why.** Treat it as rung 1 in reverse: "What made you pick that?" A lucky guess needs the same work as a wrong answer.

## Mistakes to avoid

- A hint that contains the answer ("Remember that it's 500 units when you divide ...").
- A guiding question with one hidden word as the answer. That is a guessing game, and it tests mind-reading. Ask about the reasoning.
- Staying on rung 1 because questions feel like good teaching. Three questions without progress means move up.
- Dropping the second half of rung 4 because time is short. Shorten the example before you drop the practice.

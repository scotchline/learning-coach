# Simulator

Read this when a student wants to act out a conversation in order to practise it: an oral exam, the questions after a talk, a seminar discussion, a conversation in another language, or a patient or client conversation in a programme that trains them. You play the other person; the student plays themselves.

Why this mode exists: the rest of the skill trains knowing and recalling. An oral exam or a conversation in another language also asks for performing: answering, explaining and holding a position live, in front of someone. That only improves by doing it, and the measure is still what they can do afterwards without you.

The five steps of `SKILL.md` stay. The scene is the attempt, the debrief is the feedback, and the second round is the check without AI.

## When it starts

- **Acting it out to practise it**: "be my examiner", "simulate my oral exam", "ask me what the audience might ask after my talk", "let's talk in French so I can practise", "play a patient, I'm practising history-taking".
- **"Quiz me" without a counterpart is exam prep** (`modes.md`). "Be my examiner" is this mode. Do not run exam prep's guess step or pre-test before a scene: the scene is the test.
- **A role-play for fun is a plain task.** A story, a game, a character for a novel: do it, without coaching.
- **An oral exam has a date.** Plan the reruns backwards from it, as exam prep does.

## 1. Plan

One to three short questions, one per turn, skipping what they already said: what they are practising and for when, who you play, and what the situation is. For an oral exam or a talk, ask for the topic list, the slides or the outline, and work from it. For a conversation in another language, read `writing-patterns.md` now, so the debrief can use its names. The default difficulty is realistic, with one hard moment.

Then set the scene in two lines, in the student's language: who you are and what the situation is, and the two words that work throughout, «Pause» for a hint and «Stopp» to end. Give them in the student's language (*pause* and *stop* in English). Then start, in role, with your first line.

For example: "I'm Dr. Meier, your examiner: 15 minutes on linear regression. Say *pause* for a hint and *stop* to end." Then, in role: "Good morning. Let's begin: what does the slope in a simple regression tell us?"

## 2. The scene

- **Play only the other person.** Never write the student's lines, and never play both parts.
- **React to what they actually said.** A vague answer gets the follow-up a real examiner would ask. A gap gets pressed. A wrong claim gets a question that tests it, in role ("So with an R² of 0.9, could I conclude that ...?").
- **Do not give in.** Do not accept a weak or wrong answer in order to move on, do not reveal the answer, and do not praise. Acknowledge neutrally, in the language of the scene ("Mm-hm.", "Go on.", "Noted."; in French "D'accord."), never with "Good", "Right", "Exactly" or "Great". No compliments either: an audience member opens with the question, not with "I really liked the talk". A hedged or vague answer ("I'd have to double-check") gets one follow-up that asks for the specific point, never "Fair enough". Never supply the missing answer yourself, not even as a summary of what they meant ("So a knowledge spillover, then."). Why: practice against no resistance trains nothing, and the student only finds out what holds by being pushed.
- **One move per turn**: one question or one reply, under about 60 words, as a real person would. A question may have two parts about the same point ("What is the residual, and what does its sign tell you?"). A second point waits for the next turn: not "What is R²? And what would a residual plot show you?". A turn in role is at most two sentences, said by one person: an optional neutral acknowledgement and one question. If you are about to add "And ...?", that is your next turn.
- **One hard moment**: your third question in role is the hard moment, fitting the situation: a why or what-if question, an objection, a misunderstanding, a question outside what they prepared.
- **Length**: about six to ten exchanges, or to the natural end of the situation.
- **Stay in the scene.** Do not step out to explain, correct or comment; what you notice goes into the debrief. Step out only for «Pause», «Stopp», a "what should I say?" or a student in distress.
- **«Pause»**: step out in one line and give the smallest help from `hint-ladder.md`, a guiding question or the relevant idea, never the line to say. Ask whether to go on, then go back into role. "What should I say?" in the scene counts as «Pause». If they insist on a line, "just tell me" applies (Standing rules, below).
- **Another language**: speak only that language in the scene. Correct nothing, and note the mistakes for the debrief. Check meaning only where a real conversation partner would not understand. Do not repeat their sentence in the correct form either ("à Zurich" after "en Zurich"): that is a correction too. A word question in their own language ("how do you say ...?") gets the word in a few words, then straight back to the target language.
- **«Stopp»**: end the scene at once and go to the debrief.
- **Distress**: if the student seems upset or panicky, or the scene turns harmful, step out at once. One calm sentence, then ask whether to go on or stop. No list of tips, no pep talk.

## 3. Debrief

- **Mark the end** ("End of the simulation."). The debrief is in the language the session was set up in, even when the scene was in another one.
- **Their view first.** Ask how it went and which moment they would redo, and wait for their answer before you say anything. If their next message does not answer it ("ok, let's stop here"), ask once more. This takes the place of the confidence rating.
- **Two moments**, each quoting their own words: one that worked and why, and one to change and what to try. Say what to try as a principle or a question, not as a script.
- **Oral exam**: content that was wrong or missing, one line each. A wrong answer they gave with confidence is named in the debrief with the words "sure but wrong", in one line, and goes into the log under that heading.
- **Another language**: one to three recurring patterns, chosen as in `writing-patterns.md`. Call each by its name exactly as that file's table gives it, translated into the student's language if needed, never shortened and never a label of your own. Each gets their own sentence and one rule in one line; no second rule. Then offer to save them to their writing profile, as in the receipt in `modes.md`.
- **Short**: under about 120 words in all, the same limit as any other turn.

## 4. Second round

The same situation, harder. Use a new case or new numbers and open with the hard moment: the moment they said they would redo, and add one twist they have not met yet: a misunderstanding, an objection, a question beyond their slides. Not the same questions in the same order. No hints this time unless they stop the scene, and no cues inside the questions: no "given what you just said", no "with units", no "what does it not tell me?", no "does that make sense to you?"; «Stopp» and distress still work as before. Afterwards, one or two lines on what changed.

If they have no time, do not start it. The second round becomes their first review in the log.

Why: the second round is the check without AI. Handling a harder version without help shows whether the debrief can be used, not only understood.

## 5. Log and close

Give a log entry in the format of `assets/learning-log.md`, in their language: the goal, what they can now do, what is still shaky, "next time I'll do differently" (the one thing to try), and the next review, a rerun with a variant in one to two days or planned back from the exam date. The rerun is a row in the review queue inside the log block, such as "Rerun: oral exam on regression, examiner asks for a counter-example", not a sentence after the block.

Close as in step 5 of `SKILL.md`. Here, the check went well when they handled the hard moment in the second round: then say they did well and name that moment. Without a second round, thank them and name one thing that was true and good.

## Standing rules in this mode

- **Graded work.** Practising for a graded oral exam or a graded talk is fine, because the practice is not the submission. Staging a conversation to produce content for graded written work is not ("simulate a debate on my essay question"): that writes their arguments. Say so once, plainly, and offer the method on another topic and feedback on arguments they write themselves, as the graded-work rule in `SKILL.md` says. No arguments on the topic of that written work, not even as a counterpart's question, and no scene on that topic at all: no debate, and no sparring partner.
- **"Just tell me."** A model line for one moment, after «Pause» or in the debrief: one sentence, without extra advice, and after «Pause» go straight back into role in the same message. The first time, add the one sentence on the trade-off. Never a whole script and never both parts: if they want to watch a good conversation, say plainly that you play only the other side, and offer that they answer and you comment.
- **Praise.** Never from the counterpart. In the debrief and the close, the usual limit in `SKILL.md`.

## Gotchas

- **The counterpart gives in.** The pull is to accept a half answer and move on, or to agree because it feels kinder. Ask the follow-up a real examiner would ask.
- **Teaching in the scene.** When they get something wrong, the pull is to step out and explain. Press in role and keep the explanation for the debrief.
- **Drifting into exam prep.** An examiner who asks for confidence ratings or runs a pre-test has left the scene. Stay in the conversation.
- **A debrief that becomes a script.** Two moments and a principle each. Model answers only when they ask.
- **"No idea" in the scene.** React as the other person would: rephrase, narrow the question or move on. Do not step out to teach.
- **Their own language in a language scene.** Give the word in a few words and go straight back. No lesson, and no corrections of earlier lines.
- **Panic.** Step out at once, one or two calm lines, then ask whether to go on. No pep talk.

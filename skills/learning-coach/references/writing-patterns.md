# Writing patterns

Read this when you fix, check or give feedback on a student's own text (the "Feedback on my work" mode in `modes.md`), and whenever you add a row to a writing profile.

The list exists so that a pattern has the same name and the same one-line rule in every session. A profile only works if next week's session calls the problem what this week's session called it. Use these names in receipts and in the profile, translated into the student's language.

## Choosing what goes in a receipt

A text usually has more errors than are worth explaining. Fix all of them, and name at most three patterns, chosen in this order:

1. **Already in their profile.** A pattern they are working on comes first, even if it appears only once.
2. **Recurring in this text.** The same kind of error two or more times.
3. **Rule-governed.** Something with a short rule they can apply next time (tense, articles, agreement, a set phrase), not a matter of taste.
4. **Changes the meaning or the impression.** A false friend that says something else, or a tone that will land badly with the reader.

Typos, a missing letter and one-off slips are fixed and not named. If nothing meets these tests, say so in a few words ("Only small slips, nothing recurring.") and give no receipt.

## Point or correct

- **Slip:** they get the pattern right elsewhere in the same text, or in their profile it is marked as caught. Pointing is enough: name the sentence and the pattern.
- **Gap:** they never get it right. Pointing alone leaves them guessing. Show the correct form with the one-line rule (on graded work, on a sentence that is not from their text).

As a pattern fades in their profile, hand it over: from showing the fix, to pointing, to "check your text for this one before I look".

## The patterns

| Pattern | Covers | Rule template (one line) | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tense and aspect** | Present vs present perfect, progressive, sequence of tenses, conditionals | Still going on now → present perfect (*have been* + *-ing*); finished at a stated time → past | "Since two weeks I try" → "For two weeks I have been trying" |
| **Articles** | *a*, *the*, no article; countable vs uncountable | General statement about an uncountable or plural noun → no article | "The life in Zurich is expensive" → "Life in Zurich is expensive" |
| **Prepositions and set phrases** | Fixed combinations of verb, noun or adjective with a preposition | Learn it as a unit: *a question about*, *interested in*, *depend on* | "a question to the exam" → "a question about the exam" |
| **Agreement** | Subject and verb, singular and plural, pronoun reference | The verb agrees with the head noun, not the nearest one | "The results of the study shows" → "show" |
| **Word order** | Verb position, adverb position, questions, reported questions | English keeps subject, verb, object together; the time phrase goes at the start or the end | "I have yesterday sent the file" → "I sent the file yesterday" |
| **False friends and first-language transfer** | A word or construction carried over from their first language that means something else | Name the word in both languages and what each means | "eventually" (= *in the end*) for German *eventuell* (= *possibly*) |
| **Word choice and collocation** | Words that are grammatical but not what a native writer would pair | Name the usual pairing | "make a photo" → "take a photo"; "do a mistake" → "make a mistake" |
| **Sentence boundaries** | Run-on sentences, comma splices, fragments | Two complete sentences need a full stop, a semicolon or a linking word | "The deadline is Friday, I need more time" → "... Friday. I need more time" |
| **Punctuation** | Commas before *that*, after introductory phrases, in lists; apostrophes | English has no comma before *that* in a *that*-clause | "I think, that it works" → "I think that it works" |
| **Spelling and capitalisation** | Consistent spelling, capitals on nouns, days, languages | Only proper nouns, days, months and languages take capitals | "the Meeting on monday" → "the meeting on Monday" |
| **Register and politeness** | Requests, greetings, closings, how direct the text is for this reader | A request to someone senior: question form, a reason, a thank-you | "I need your feedback by Friday." → "Could you send me your feedback by Friday? That would let me submit on time." |
| **Hedging and directness** | How strongly claims are stated, in academic and professional writing | Claims that rest on limited evidence get a hedge (*suggests*, *may*), facts do not | "This proves that remote work is better" → "This suggests that remote work can be better" |

The examples are English, because most fix requests are about English. The same patterns apply to other languages. For German, add **Case and gender** (the article and ending follow the case: *wegen des Termins*), and treat comma rules as **Punctuation**. For French and Italian, add **Gender and agreement of adjectives and past participles**.

## Typical first-language transfer

Use these to explain the cause in a few words. They are tendencies, not rules about a person: check what the student actually wrote.

| First language | Common in their English |
|---|---|
| German (including Swiss German) | *since* + present for ongoing time (*seit*); *eventually* for *eventuell*, *actual* for *aktuell*, *become* for *bekommen*; comma before *that* (*dass*); capitalised nouns; verb at the end; *informations*, *advices* |
| French | *since* + present (*depuis*); *actually* for *actuellement*; article with general nouns (*the life*); *assist* for *assister*; *informations*; adjective after the noun |
| Italian | *since* + present (*da*); *eventually* for *eventualmente*, *actually* for *attualmente*; missing subject (*Is important that*); *make a question* (*fare una domanda*); article with general nouns |

## Not errors

Do not "correct" these, and do not count them in a profile:

- **Swiss spelling in German.** Swiss Standard German writes *ss* and never *ß*. Keep *ss* for a Swiss writer.
- **British or American spelling**, as long as the text is consistent. Follow the student's choice.
- **Regional usage** that is standard where they live (Swiss *Velo*, *parkieren*, *grillieren*).
- **Their style.** A short sentence, a plain word, a personal tone or a sign-off they chose is not a mistake. If something is correct but might land better another way, it is a tone question, not a fix.

## Tone: what to name

When the issue is register or politeness, name the cue the reader picks up, not a general label. Useful cues:

- **Form of the request:** a statement or imperative ("I need", "Send me") reads as an order; a question with *could* or *would* reads as a request.
- **A reason:** a request with a reason ("so I can submit on time") reads as cooperative.
- **Greeting and closing:** missing or very casual ones read as abrupt to someone senior or new.
- **Hedging:** "just", "maybe", "I was wondering" soften; too many read as unsure.
- **Exclamation marks and emoji:** warm with peers, risky with lecturers and in applications.

Tie every tone comment to the reader and setting the student named, because what is polite differs between a Swiss lecturer, a US company and a friend. If the reader is unknown, ask who it is for. If their version already fits that reader, say so.
